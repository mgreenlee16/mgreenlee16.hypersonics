Lesson 3 · Part I

Nozzles, Diffusers & Choked Flow

Why must a nozzle converge and then diverge to make supersonic flow? What happens when the downstream pressure is too high, or too low? Quasi-one-dimensional flow answers both questions with surprisingly simple algebra, and gives the design rules for every supersonic nozzle, diffuser and wind tunnel.

Section 3.1Quasi-one-dimensional flow

In a duct whose area \(A(x)\) changes gradually, the flow properties vary mainly along the axis. We assume they are uniform across each section and depend only on \(x\). This is quasi-one-dimensional flow. It is an approximation, since the walls turn the flow and so there must be some transverse velocity, but it is an excellent one for slender ducts. Applying the integral conservation laws to a slice of the duct between stations 1 and 2:

$$\rho_1u_1A_1 = \rho_2u_2A_2$$ $$p_1A_1 + \rho_1u_1^2A_1 + \int_{A_1}^{A_2}p\,dA = p_2A_2 + \rho_2u_2^2A_2$$ $$h_1 + \frac{u_1^2}{2} = h_2 + \frac{u_2^2}{2}$$

The integral is the axial force exerted by the walls on the gas. In differential form, \(d(\rho uA) = 0\), \(dp = -\rho u\,du\) (Euler's equation, the same as in truly one-dimensional flow) and \(dh + u\,du = 0\). The flow is isentropic away from shocks, so the isentropic relations of Lesson 1 hold along the duct.

Section 3.2The area–velocity relation

Write continuity logarithmically, \(d\rho/\rho + du/u + dA/A = 0\). Then eliminate \(d\rho\) with \(dp = a^2d\rho\) and Euler's equation, \(d\rho/\rho = -M^2\,du/u\):

$$\boxed{\frac{dA}{A} = \left(M^2 - 1\right)\frac{du}{u}}$$
  • Subsonic (M < 1): velocity rises when the area falls, as in a garden-hose nozzle. Density changes only slightly, so continuity is dominated by velocity.
  • Supersonic (M > 1): velocity rises when the area grows. The density falls faster than the velocity rises, so the area must grow to carry the same mass flow.
  • Sonic (M = 1): \(dA/A = 0\), so M = 1 can occur only at a minimum (or maximum) area. In a smooth accelerating flow, that means a throat.

So the only way to expand a gas smoothly from rest to supersonic speed is to make it converge to a throat where M = 1 and then diverge. This is the de Laval nozzle. Read backwards, the relation describes a diffuser: to slow a supersonic flow the duct must converge, and once the flow is subsonic it must diverge. Section 3.3The area–Mach relation

Take \(A^*\) as the area where the flow would be sonic. Continuity between a general section and the sonic one, \(\rho uA = \rho^*a^*A^*\), with the isentropic relations, gives

$$\left(\frac{A}{A^*}\right)^2 = \frac{1}{M^2}\left[\frac{2}{\gamma+1}\left(1 + \frac{\gamma-1}{2}M^2\right)\right]^{\frac{\gamma+1}{\gamma-1}}.$$

Three facts follow. \(A/A^* \ge 1\) always, with equality only at M = 1. For each \(A/A^* > 1\) there are two solutions, one subsonic and one supersonic. Which one the flow takes depends on the pressures imposed at the ends of the duct. And \(A^*\) need not exist physically. It is a reference area, which changes across a shock: since \(\rho^*a^* \propto p_0\), the product \(p_0A^*\) is constant for a choked flow, and \(A^*_2/A^*_1 = p_{0,1}/p_{0,2}\) across a shock.

Section 3.4Mass flow and choking

The mass flow through any section, written with total conditions, is

$$\dot m = \rho uA = \frac{p_0A}{\sqrt{T_0}}\sqrt{\frac{\gamma}{R}}\,M\left(1 + \frac{\gamma-1}{2}M^2\right)^{-\frac{\gamma+1}{2(\gamma-1)}}.$$

For fixed \(p_0, T_0\), the mass flux \(\rho u\) is greatest at M = 1. Subsonic acceleration raises velocity faster than density falls; supersonic acceleration does the opposite. When the throat becomes sonic, the mass flow is the largest the duct can pass and the nozzle is choked:

$$\dot m_\text{max} = \frac{p_0A^*}{\sqrt{T_0}}\sqrt{\frac{\gamma}{R}\left(\frac{2}{\gamma+1}\right)^{\frac{\gamma+1}{\gamma-1}}} \approx 0.0404\,\frac{p_0A^*}{\sqrt{T_0}}\ \ (\text{air, SI}).$$

Once the throat is sonic, lowering the back pressure further cannot raise the flow. The news that \(p_b\) has changed travels upstream at \(a - u\), which is zero at the throat. Raising \(p_0\) or cooling the reservoir still increases the mass flow. Choked flow is used everywhere: critical-flow venturis meter gases accurately, safety valves and orifices limit flow, and turbine nozzle guide vanes set engine mass flow.

Section 3.5Designing a nozzle

A nozzle design starts from the required exit Mach number \(M_e\), reservoir conditions and mass flow. The steps are:

  1. Throat area from the choked mass flow: \(A^* = \dot m\sqrt{T_0}/(0.0404\,p_0)\).
  2. Exit area from the area–Mach relation: \(A_e = A^*\,(A/A^*)_{M_e}\).
  3. Design (perfectly expanded) back pressure: \(p_e = p_0/(p_0/p)_{M_e}\).
  4. Contour. For uniform, parallel exit flow, as in a wind tunnel, the diverging wall is shaped by the method of characteristics so that the expansion waves generated near the throat are cancelled at the wall. A minimum-length nozzle concentrates all the expansion at a sharp-cornered throat. Practical nozzles use a smooth expansion region followed by a straightening section. A simple cone diverges the flow and leaves it non-uniform at the exit.

Section 3.6The converging–diverging nozzle at every back pressure

Fix the reservoir at \(p_0\) and slowly lower the back pressure \(p_b\). Three critical pressures, all set by \(A_e/A_t\) and γ, divide the behavior:

  1. \(p_b > p_{e,1}\): subsonic throughout. The flow accelerates to the throat and decelerates after it, like a venturi. The throat is not sonic, and the mass flow depends on \(p_b\).
  2. \(p_b = p_{e,1}\): choked, subsonic exit. The throat just reaches M = 1; the diverging section stays on the subsonic branch.
  3. \(p_{e,2} < p_b < p_{e,1}\): normal shock inside the diverging section. Supersonic from the throat to the shock, subsonic after it, with \(p_e = p_b\). Its location is found by requiring that the subsonic flow behind it, with the new reference area \(A_2^* = A_t\,p_{0,1}/p_{0,2}\), exits at exactly \(p_b\). As \(p_b\) falls, the shock moves downstream and strengthens.
  4. \(p_b = p_{e,2}\): normal shock at the exit plane.
  5. \(p_{e,3} < p_b < p_{e,2}\): over-expanded. The nozzle flow is fully supersonic and shock-free, and \(p_e = p_{e,3} < p_b\). Oblique shocks at the lip raise the jet pressure to \(p_b\).
  6. \(p_b = p_{e,3}\): design condition (perfectly expanded): the jet leaves parallel, at ambient pressure.
  7. \(p_b < p_{e,3}\): under-expanded. Expansion fans at the lip lower the jet pressure to \(p_b\).

From case 2 onward the mass flow is fixed at its choked value, and nothing downstream of the throat can change it.

Section 3.7Over- and under-expanded jets

A supersonic jet that leaves at the wrong pressure adjusts outside the nozzle through waves anchored at the lip. With the oblique-shock and Prandtl–Meyer relations (Lessons 5 and 6), the first wave can be computed exactly.

  • Over-expanded (\(p_e < p_b\)): an oblique shock from the lip must raise the pressure by \(p_b/p_e\). That fixes its normal Mach number, \(M_e\sin\beta\), and so its angle β and the inward deflection θ of the jet boundary. If \(p_b/p_e\) exceeds the normal-shock ratio at \(M_e\), no oblique shock is strong enough, and the shock moves into the nozzle (case 3 above). For moderately strong lip shocks, regular reflection at the axis becomes impossible, and a Mach disk forms.
  • Under-expanded (\(p_e > p_b\)): a Prandtl–Meyer fan at the lip expands the flow to \(p_b\), and the boundary turns outward by \(\theta = \nu(M_2) - \nu(M_e)\).

These waves reflect from the axis and from the constant-pressure jet boundary. A shock reflects from a free boundary as an expansion and vice versa, so the pattern repeats as the familiar train of "shock diamonds", until viscous mixing erodes it.

Section 3.8Real-nozzle effects

  • Boundary layers. Viscous layers on the walls displace the core flow, reducing the effective area by the displacement thickness δ*. A nozzle designed for M = 3 from inviscid theory delivers a lower Mach number unless its contour is corrected. Wind-tunnel nozzles are designed with an inviscid contour plus δ*.
  • Separation. In a strongly over-expanded nozzle the boundary layer cannot withstand the adverse pressure gradient of the lip shock. It separates inside the nozzle, typically when \(p_e/p_b\) falls below about 0.3–0.4 (the Summerfield criterion). The shock then sits inside the nozzle as an oblique, λ-shaped structure, and the flow can become unsteady and asymmetric.
  • Non-uniform exit flow. A conical nozzle with half-angle α delivers flow that diverges from the axis, so the axial momentum flux is reduced by the factor \((1 + \cos\alpha)/2\). Contoured nozzles avoid this.
  • Throat discharge coefficient. Curvature at the throat makes the sonic line curved, and boundary layers reduce the effective throat area. Real mass flows are typically 0.98–0.995 of the ideal.

Section 3.9Diffusers

A diffuser decelerates a stream while recovering as much total pressure as possible. Reversing an ideal nozzle would bring a supersonic flow isentropically to M = 1 at a throat and then to rest, recovering all of \(p_0\). In practice this is impossible. The required throat is too small to start (the initial normal shock cannot be swallowed), and the isentropic solution is unstable to any disturbance. A real supersonic diffuser uses a converging section to weaken the flow, a weak normal shock near the throat, and a subsonic diverging section. Its figure of merit is the total-pressure recovery \(p_{0,\text{exit}}/p_{0,\infty}\), compared with a single normal shock at the flight Mach number. Starting and second-throat sizing are worked out in detail in Lesson 8.

Section 3.10Worked examples

Worked examples

Air expands isentropically from a reservoir at \(p_0, T_0\) through a C-D nozzle with throat area \(A_t\) and exit area ratio \(A_e/A_t\). Find the exit Mach number, pressure, temperature, velocity and mass flow for the design (supersonic) condition.

The same kind of nozzle exhausts to a back pressure \(p_b\) between \(p_{e,2}\) and \(p_{e,1}\), so a normal shock stands inside. Find where it stands and the exit state.

Method

Behind the shock the flow is subsonic with the new reference area A₂* = A_t/(p₀₂/p₀₁). The exit satisfies pₑ = p_b and p₀ₑ = p₀₂, so (pₑAₑ)/(p₀₁A_t) = (p/p₀)(A/A*) at the exit, a quantity that depends only on Mₑ. Solve it for Mₑ, then get p₀₂/p₀₁ = (pₑ/p₀₁)(p₀/p)ₑ, and from that the shock Mach number and its location.

A converging nozzle of exit area \(A_e\) is supplied from \(p_0, T_0\) and exhausts to \(p_b\). Is it choked? Find the exit state and the mass flow.

SummaryKey equations

Area–velocity and area–Mach

$$\frac{dA}{A} = (M^2 - 1)\frac{du}{u}$$ $$\left(\frac{A}{A^*}\right)^2 = \frac{1}{M^2}\left[\frac{2}{\gamma+1}\left(1 + \frac{\gamma-1}{2}M^2\right)\right]^{\frac{\gamma+1}{\gamma-1}}$$

Choked mass flow

$$\dot m = \frac{p_0A^*}{\sqrt{T_0}}\sqrt{\frac{\gamma}{R}\left(\frac{2}{\gamma+1}\right)^{\frac{\gamma+1}{\gamma-1}}}$$ $$p_0A^* = \text{const across a shock}$$

Critical back pressures

$$p_{e,1}: \text{subsonic root},\quad p_{e,3}: \text{supersonic root}$$ $$p_{e,2} = p_{e,3}\left[1 + \frac{2\gamma}{\gamma+1}(M_e^2 - 1)\right]$$

Jet boundary at the lip

$$\text{over-expanded: } M_e\sin\beta = M_n,\ \frac{p_b}{p_e} = 1 + \frac{2\gamma}{\gamma+1}(M_n^2-1)$$ $$\text{under-expanded: } \theta = \nu(M_2) - \nu(M_e)$$
