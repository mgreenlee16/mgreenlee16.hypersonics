Lesson 2 · Part I

Normal Shock Waves

A shock wave is a region a few mean free paths thick across which a supersonic flow is compressed almost discontinuously. A normal shock stands perpendicular to the flow. It is the simplest shock to analyze, and the building block for oblique shocks, Pitot probes, diffusers, nozzles and wind tunnels.

Section 2.1How shocks form

Sound waves are how a body tells the oncoming flow it is there. In subsonic flow those signals travel upstream, and the flow begins to adjust well ahead of the body. In supersonic flow they cannot outrun the stream. The adjustment is postponed until it happens abruptly, across a shock.

There is a second, more mechanical way to see it. A finite compression wave is made of many small pressure increments. Each increment compresses and heats the gas slightly, so the next one travels through warmer gas, at a higher sound speed, and moving downstream with the gas that has been set in motion. For a simple wave in one dimension, each point on the wave profile moves at

$$\frac{dx}{dt} = u + a = a_1 + \frac{\gamma+1}{2}u.$$

Points of higher velocity (higher pressure) move faster. The back of a compression catches up with the front, the profile steepens, and eventually it would become multivalued. Before that can happen, the gradients become so steep that viscosity and heat conduction intervene, and a thin, steady shock forms. An expansion does the opposite and spreads out, which is why there are no expansion shocks. Interactive 1 follows this process for a smooth pulse.

Section 2.2The governing equations

Take a control volume enclosing a stationary normal shock, with uniform state 1 upstream and state 2 downstream. The flow is steady and adiabatic, with no body forces, and the side faces carry no shear. The integral equations of Lesson 1 reduce to

$$\rho_1u_1 = \rho_2u_2 \qquad\text{(continuity)}$$ $$p_1 + \rho_1u_1^2 = p_2 + \rho_2u_2^2 \qquad\text{(momentum)}$$ $$h_1 + \frac{u_1^2}{2} = h_2 + \frac{u_2^2}{2} \qquad\text{(energy)}$$

With \(p = \rho RT\) and \(h = c_pT\), these are five equations in five unknowns. Two features stand out. Momentum says that \(p + \rho u^2\), the "impulse", is conserved. Energy says total enthalpy, and so \(T_0\), is the same on both sides.

Section 2.3The Prandtl relation

Divide the momentum equation by the continuity equation:

$$\frac{p_1}{\rho_1u_1} - \frac{p_2}{\rho_2u_2} = u_2 - u_1 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{a_1^2}{\gamma u_1} - \frac{a_2^2}{\gamma u_2} = u_2 - u_1.$$

Express \(a_1^2\) and \(a_2^2\) through the energy equation as \(a^2 = \tfrac{\gamma+1}{2}a^{*2} - \tfrac{\gamma-1}{2}u^2\), where \(a^*\) is common to both sides because the flow is adiabatic. Everything collapses to

$$u_1u_2 = a^{*2} \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad M_1^*M_2^* = 1.$$

This is the Prandtl relation. If the upstream flow is supersonic (\(M_1^* > 1\)), the downstream flow must be subsonic (\(M_2^* < 1\)), and vice versa.

Section 2.4The normal-shock relations

Using \(M^{*2} = (\gamma+1)M^2/[2 + (\gamma-1)M^2]\) in the Prandtl relation, and then continuity and momentum, gives every downstream quantity as a function of \(M_1\) and γ alone:

$$M_2^2 = \frac{1 + \frac{\gamma-1}{2}M_1^2}{\gamma M_1^2 - \frac{\gamma-1}{2}}$$ $$\frac{\rho_2}{\rho_1} = \frac{u_1}{u_2} = \frac{(\gamma+1)M_1^2}{2 + (\gamma-1)M_1^2}$$ $$\frac{p_2}{p_1} = 1 + \frac{2\gamma}{\gamma+1}\left(M_1^2 - 1\right)$$ $$\frac{T_2}{T_1} = \frac{h_2}{h_1} = \frac{p_2}{p_1}\frac{\rho_1}{\rho_2}$$

The quantity \(M_1^2 - 1\) appears throughout. It is a natural measure of shock strength, and \((p_2 - p_1)/p_1 = \tfrac{2\gamma}{\gamma+1}(M_1^2 - 1)\) is often called the shock strength itself.

Section 2.5The Hugoniot curve

Eliminating the velocities from the three conservation equations leaves a relation among thermodynamic properties only, the Hugoniot equation:

$$e_2 - e_1 = \frac{p_1 + p_2}{2}\left(v_1 - v_2\right).$$

It looks like the first law with the average pressure doing the work. For a calorically perfect gas it gives a curve in the p–v plane:

$$\frac{p_2}{p_1} = \frac{\frac{\gamma+1}{\gamma-1} - \frac{v_2}{v_1}}{\frac{\gamma+1}{\gamma-1}\frac{v_2}{v_1} - 1}.$$

Continuity and momentum alone give a straight line through state 1, the Rayleigh line, with slope \(-(\rho_1u_1)^2\). In nondimensional form, \(p_2/p_1 - 1 = -\gamma M_1^2(v_2/v_1 - 1)\). The shock jumps from state 1 to the intersection of the two. Comparing the Hugoniot with the isentrope \(p v^\gamma = \text{const}\) shows the essential physics. For the same compression \(v_2/v_1\), a shock reaches a higher pressure (and temperature) than an isentropic compression. The difference is the entropy it has produced. The two curves touch at state 1, with equal slope and curvature, which is why weak shocks are nearly isentropic.

Section 2.6Entropy and the direction of the jump

The normal-shock relations are equally satisfied by \(M_1 < 1\), which would describe an "expansion shock" that accelerates a subsonic flow to supersonic. The second law rules this out. The entropy change across the shock is

$$\frac{s_2 - s_1}{R} = \frac{\gamma}{\gamma-1}\ln\frac{T_2}{T_1} - \ln\frac{p_2}{p_1} = -\ln\frac{p_{0,2}}{p_{0,1}},$$

and it is negative for every \(M_1 < 1\). Shocks can only compress. Expanding the relation for weak shocks gives

$$\frac{s_2 - s_1}{R} \approx \frac{2\gamma}{(\gamma+1)^2}\frac{\left(M_1^2-1\right)^3}{3}.$$

Entropy rises only as the cube of the shock strength. That is why weak shocks are almost isentropic, and why compressing a flow through several weak shocks loses far less total pressure than one strong shock (Lesson 5). The total-pressure ratio is

$$\frac{p_{0,2}}{p_{0,1}} = \left[\frac{(\gamma+1)M_1^2}{2+(\gamma-1)M_1^2}\right]^{\frac{\gamma}{\gamma-1}}\left[\frac{\gamma+1}{2\gamma M_1^2-(\gamma-1)}\right]^{\frac{1}{\gamma-1}}.$$

Section 2.7Weak and strong limits

As \(M_1 \to 1\), every ratio tends to 1 and the shock degenerates into a Mach wave, an infinitesimal sound wave. As \(M_1 \to \infty\), still with constant γ:

$$M_2 \to \sqrt{\frac{\gamma-1}{2\gamma}} = 0.378, \qquad \frac{\rho_2}{\rho_1} \to \frac{\gamma+1}{\gamma-1} = 6, \qquad \frac{p_2}{p_1} \approx \frac{2\gamma}{\gamma+1}M_1^2, \qquad \frac{T_2}{T_1} \approx \frac{2\gamma(\gamma-1)}{(\gamma+1)^2}M_1^2.$$

The density ratio is bounded: however strong the shock, a perfect gas with γ = 1.4 is compressed by at most a factor of six, and the excess energy goes into heating. At very high Mach numbers real air absorbs energy in molecular vibration and dissociation, which these relations do not include.

Section 2.8Inside the shock

The jump conditions say nothing about what happens within the shock. Navier–Stokes theory (Becker, 1922) and kinetic theory both give a smooth but very steep profile. Its thickness δ is a few mean free paths λ for moderate shocks. For air at sea level λ ≈ 0.07 μm, so a shock is roughly 0.1–0.3 μm thick. A useful estimate is

$$\frac{\delta}{\lambda_1} \sim \frac{4}{M_1 - 1}\quad (M_1 \text{ near } 1),$$

which approaches a few mean free paths by M₁ ≈ 2. For strong shocks the continuum equations are no longer accurate inside the shock, but the jump conditions remain exact, because they follow from conservation alone. In practice shocks are thin enough to be surfaces of discontinuity in every aerodynamic calculation, except in very rarefied flow.

Section 2.9Pitot probes

A Pitot tube is an open-ended tube facing the flow. It brings the gas at its mouth to rest and reads the pressure there.

Subsonic flow

The deceleration is isentropic, so the probe reads \(p_0\). With a separate static-pressure measurement, the Mach number follows from the isentropic relation:

$$M_1^2 = \frac{2}{\gamma-1}\left[\left(\frac{p_0}{p_1}\right)^{(\gamma-1)/\gamma} - 1\right].$$

Supersonic flow

A detached bow shock stands ahead of the probe. The small part of it in front of the mouth is locally normal, so the probe reads the total pressure behind a normal shock, \(p_{0,2}\). Combining the normal-shock relations with the isentropic deceleration behind it gives the Rayleigh Pitot-tube formula:

$$\frac{p_{0,2}}{p_1} = \left[\frac{(\gamma+1)^2M_1^2}{4\gamma M_1^2 - 2(\gamma-1)}\right]^{\frac{\gamma}{\gamma-1}}\frac{1-\gamma+2\gamma M_1^2}{\gamma+1}.$$

It is implicit in \(M_1\) but monotonic, so it is easily inverted numerically. Applying the subsonic formula to a supersonic Pitot reading gives a badly wrong answer. In wind tunnels, the test-section Mach number is often found from the ratio of Pitot pressure to the reservoir pressure, \(p_{0,2}/p_{0,1}\), which is just the normal-shock total-pressure ratio (Lesson 8).

Section 2.10Moving shocks

A shock moving at speed \(W\) into still gas looks stationary to an observer riding with it. That observer sees gas approaching at \(W\). The stationary relations therefore apply with the shock Mach number \(M_s = W/a_1\) in place of \(M_1\). Back in the laboratory frame, the gas behind the shock is set in motion after it with the induced velocity

$$u_p = W\left(1 - \frac{\rho_1}{\rho_2}\right) = \frac{a_1}{\gamma}\left(\frac{p_2}{p_1} - 1\right)\sqrt{\frac{\frac{2\gamma}{\gamma+1}}{\frac{p_2}{p_1} + \frac{\gamma-1}{\gamma+1}}}, \qquad M_s = \sqrt{\frac{\gamma+1}{2\gamma}\left(\frac{p_2}{p_1} - 1\right) + 1}.$$

Static properties are the same in both frames. Total properties are not, because the velocity differs. A moving shock raises the total temperature of the gas it overtakes, since the shock does work on it. Shock tubes use this effect to produce short bursts of hot, high-pressure gas for chemical kinetics and aerodynamic testing. When the gas behind the shock moves faster than its own sound speed (\(u_p > a_2\), which needs \(p_2/p_1\) above about 4.8 for air), the induced flow is itself supersonic.

SummaryKey equations

Conservation across the shock

$$\rho_1u_1 = \rho_2u_2,\quad p_1 + \rho_1u_1^2 = p_2 + \rho_2u_2^2$$ $$h_1 + \tfrac12u_1^2 = h_2 + \tfrac12u_2^2,\qquad u_1u_2 = a^{*2}$$

Jump relations

$$M_2^2 = \frac{1 + \frac{\gamma-1}{2}M_1^2}{\gamma M_1^2 - \frac{\gamma-1}{2}},\quad \frac{p_2}{p_1} = 1 + \frac{2\gamma}{\gamma+1}(M_1^2-1)$$ $$\frac{\rho_2}{\rho_1} = \frac{(\gamma+1)M_1^2}{2 + (\gamma-1)M_1^2}$$

Entropy

$$\frac{s_2 - s_1}{R} = -\ln\frac{p_{0,2}}{p_{0,1}} \approx \frac{2\gamma}{(\gamma+1)^2}\frac{(M_1^2-1)^3}{3}$$ $$T_{0,2} = T_{0,1}$$

Rayleigh Pitot formula

$$\frac{p_{0,2}}{p_1} = \left[\frac{(\gamma+1)^2M_1^2}{4\gamma M_1^2 - 2(\gamma-1)}\right]^{\frac{\gamma}{\gamma-1}}\frac{1-\gamma+2\gamma M_1^2}{\gamma+1}$$
