Lesson 11 · Part V

High-Temperature Gas Dynamics

A vehicle entering from orbit at 7.8 km/s carries about 30 MJ of kinetic energy per kilogram of air it meets. That is enough to vibrate, dissociate and even ionize the air in its shock layer. The gas then stops behaving like a perfect gas with γ = 1.4: shock temperatures fall by a factor of several, densities rise, and pressure distributions shift in ways that once caught the Space Shuttle by surprise. This lesson computes air in chemical equilibrium from its molecular thermodynamics, solves real-gas shocks, and introduces nonequilibrium.

Section 11.1Why the perfect-gas model fails

The calorically perfect gas assumes constant specific heats, which is true only while the molecules translate and rotate. Apply it to a normal shock at 11 km/s and 52 km altitude (lunar return) and it predicts 58,000 K behind the shock. The real figure is about 11,600 K. Most of the energy goes into breaking molecules apart rather than into raising the temperature. For air, the thresholds (at about 1 atm) are roughly:

TemperatureWhat happensConsequence
≳ 800 KVibration of N₂ and O₂ becomes excitedcp rises; γ falls below 1.4 (thermally perfect gas)
≈ 2,000–4,000 KO₂ dissociates: O₂ → 2O; some NO formsGas becomes a reacting mixture; molar mass falls
≈ 4,000–9,000 KN₂ dissociates: N₂ → 2NLarge energy absorption; shock temperatures much lower than perfect-gas values
≳ 9,000 KIonization: N → N⁺ + e⁻, O → O⁺ + e⁻ (NO⁺ first, at lower temperature)Plasma: radio blackout, radiative heating

Lower pressure shifts all of these to lower temperatures, because dissociation increases the number of particles, and low pressure favors that side of the reaction.

Section 11.2Molecular energy and vibration

Statistical mechanics assigns each molecule translational, rotational, vibrational and electronic energy. Translation contributes \(\tfrac32 RT\) per unit mass, and rotation of a diatomic molecule another \(RT\), both fully excited at room temperature. Vibration, modeled as a harmonic oscillator with characteristic temperature \(\theta_v = h\nu/k\), contributes

$$e_\text{vib} = \frac{R\theta_v}{e^{\theta_v/T} - 1}, \qquad \frac{c_v}{R} = \frac52 + \left(\frac{\theta_v}{T}\right)^2\frac{e^{\theta_v/T}}{\left(e^{\theta_v/T}-1\right)^2}.$$

With \(\theta_v\) = 3,394 K for N₂ and 2,274 K for O₂, vibration is negligible at 300 K but approaches its classical value \(c_v = \tfrac72R\) above about 2θv. γ then falls from 7/5 toward 9/7 = 1.286. A gas with \(c_p(T)\) but no chemistry is thermally perfect. Section 11.3Chemical equilibrium of air

In chemical equilibrium, each reaction balances. For a dissociation such as O₂ ⇌ 2O, the law of mass action relates the partial pressures through an equilibrium constant that depends only on temperature:

$$K_p(T) = \frac{(p_\text{O}/p^\circ)^2}{p_{\text{O}_2}/p^\circ} = \exp\!\left(-\frac{\Delta G^\circ(T)}{\mathcal R T}\right), \qquad \Delta G^\circ = 2g^\circ_\text{O} - g^\circ_{\text{O}_2}.$$

Here \(g^\circ = h^\circ - Ts^\circ\) is the molar Gibbs energy at the standard pressure \(p^\circ\) = 1 bar. This site treats air as 11 species (N₂, O₂, NO, N, O, N₂⁺, O₂⁺, NO⁺, N⁺, O⁺, e⁻). The thermodynamic functions of each come from the NASA Glenn nine-coefficient polynomials (McBride, Zehe & Gordon, NASA TP-2002-211556), valid from 200 K to 20,000 K. Writing every species in terms of N and O atoms and electrons, and adding conservation of the N:O ratio, the total pressure and charge neutrality, leaves two unknowns (the partial pressures of atomic N and O). These are solved by Newton's method in logarithmic variables.

Section 11.4Properties of equilibrium air

Two summary numbers describe how far equilibrium air departs from a perfect gas. The compressibility \(Z = p/(\rho R_\text{air}T)\) counts how many particles there are relative to undissociated air: it rises from 1 toward 2 as the molecules split, and beyond 2 as ionization frees electrons. The effective γ, \(\tilde\gamma = h/e\), is the γ that a perfect gas would need to have the same \(h\) and \(e\) at that state. It falls to about 1.1–1.2 in dissociating air. Many engineering methods, from Newtonian estimates to the Shuttle analysis in Section 11.7, use \(\tilde\gamma\) as a shortcut to real-gas effects.

The equilibrium speed of sound is lower than the frozen one, because a sound wave in equilibrium air shifts the chemistry as it passes. The calculators report both.

Section 11.5The equilibrium normal shock

The normal-shock conservation equations hold whatever the gas:

$$\rho_1u_1 = \rho_2u_2,\qquad p_1 + \rho_1u_1^2 = p_2 + \rho_2u_2^2,\qquad h_1 + \tfrac12u_1^2 = h_2 + \tfrac12u_2^2.$$

With \(\varepsilon = \rho_1/\rho_2\) they become \(p_2 = p_1 + \rho_1u_1^2(1-\varepsilon)\) and \(h_2 = h_1 + \tfrac12u_1^2(1-\varepsilon^2)\). Guess ε, compute \(p_2\) and \(h_2\), find the equilibrium state with that enthalpy and pressure, update ε, and repeat (Anderson, Hypersonic and High-Temperature Gas Dynamics, ch. 14). Because \(p_2\) depends only weakly on ε, it is close to the perfect-gas value, but \(T_2\) and \(\rho_2\) are very different. The density ratio exceeds the perfect-gas limit of 6, reaching 15–20 at entry speeds.

Section 11.6Nonequilibrium and relaxation

Equilibrium assumes the molecules have had enough collisions to settle. Right behind a shock they have not. The shock itself is only a few mean free paths thick, so it raises the translational and rotational temperature at once while vibration and chemistry lag. Behind it is a relaxation zone in which vibration is excited, molecules dissociate, and the temperature falls from its "frozen" value (the perfect-gas γ = 1.4 result) toward equilibrium.

Whether a flow is in equilibrium is decided by the Damköhler number, the ratio of flow time to chemical (or relaxation) time:

$$Da = \frac{\tau_\text{flow}}{\tau_\text{chem}} = \frac{L/u}{\tau}.\qquad Da \gg 1:\ \text{equilibrium};\quad Da \ll 1:\ \text{frozen}.$$

For vibrational relaxation, the Millikan–White correlation gives \(p\tau = \exp[A(T^{-1/3} - 0.015\,\mu^{1/4}) - 18.42]\) atm·s, with \(A = 1.16\times10^{-3}\mu^{1/2}\theta_v^{4/3}\) and μ the reduced mass in atomic mass units (14 for N₂–N₂). Since τ ∝ 1/p, the relaxation length ∝ 1/ρ. Dissociation reactions are dominated by two-body collisions, so the same holds: nonequilibrium flows are similar when the product \(\rho L\) is matched. This binary scaling lets a small model at high density reproduce the chemistry of a large vehicle at high altitude, and it is the basis of reflected-shock tunnel testing (Lesson 15).

Expanding nozzles freeze the other way. In the throat of a high-enthalpy tunnel the gas is near equilibrium, but it expands so fast that recombination cannot keep up. The test section is then left with dissociated, "frozen" oxygen atoms that are not present in flight.

Section 11.7The Shuttle's hypersonic pitching-moment anomaly

On the first Space Shuttle flight, STS-1 in April 1981, the Orbiter needed a body-flap deflection roughly twice the preflight prediction to trim at hypersonic speeds. The aerodynamic database, built mainly from cold-gas wind-tunnel tests near Mach 6–8, had underpredicted the nose-up pitching moment. Maus and co-workers (1984) traced most of the discrepancy to real-gas effects, a case study that Hirschel and Weiland treat at length.

The mechanism is captured by an effective γ. On the windward side the flow passes a strong compression at the nose and forebody, expands over the aft lower surface, and is compressed again by the body flap. In dissociating air the effective γ is lower. A lower γ reduces the pressure that survives the expansion onto the aft surface, relative to the forebody pressure. Less pressure aft of the center of gravity means less nose-down moment, so the vehicle pitches nose-up and the flap must deflect further to restore trim. A cold wind tunnel with γ = 1.4 cannot show this, however well it matches the Mach number.

Real vehicles show other high-temperature effects too. Heat-shield surfaces can be catalytic, recombining atoms at the wall and releasing the dissociation energy as extra heating (Lesson 12). Ionization produces the communications blackout of re-entry. And above about 10 km/s, radiation from the hot shock layer becomes a significant part of the heating of large capsules.
