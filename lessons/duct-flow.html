Lesson 4 · Part I

Heat Addition & Friction in Ducts

Area change is only one way to change the Mach number of a duct flow. Adding heat and wall friction are two more. Both push the flow toward M = 1, from either side, and both can choke the duct. The idealized cases are named after Rayleigh (heat, no friction) and Fanno (friction, no heat).

1. Rayleigh flow: constant-area heat addition

Consider steady, inviscid flow in a constant-area duct, with heat \(q\) per unit mass added between stations 1 and 2, from combustion, a heater, or condensation. Continuity and momentum are unchanged from the normal shock, and only energy picks up a source term:

$$\rho_1u_1 = \rho_2u_2, \qquad p_1 + \rho_1u_1^2 = p_2 + \rho_2u_2^2, \qquad q = c_p\left(T_{0,2} - T_{0,1}\right).$$

So heat addition is, by definition, a change of total temperature. Using \(\rho u^2 = \gamma p M^2\), momentum gives the pressure ratio at once, and the rest follows:

$$\frac{p_2}{p_1} = \frac{1+\gamma M_1^2}{1+\gamma M_2^2}, \qquad \frac{T_2}{T_1} = \left(\frac{1+\gamma M_1^2}{1+\gamma M_2^2}\right)^2\frac{M_2^2}{M_1^2},$$ $$\frac{T_{0,2}}{T_{0,1}} = \frac{(1+\gamma M_1^2)^2}{(1+\gamma M_2^2)^2}\frac{M_2^2}{M_1^2}\frac{1+\frac{\gamma-1}{2}M_2^2}{1+\frac{\gamma-1}{2}M_1^2}.$$

2. Starred reference conditions

As with the area–Mach relation, it is convenient to refer every state to the one where the flow would reach M = 1 under the same process, here by adding just enough heat. Setting \(M_2 = 1\):

$$\frac{p}{p^*} = \frac{1+\gamma}{1+\gamma M^2}, \qquad \frac{T}{T^*} = \frac{(1+\gamma)^2M^2}{(1+\gamma M^2)^2}, \qquad \frac{\rho^*}{\rho} = \frac{u}{u^*} = \frac{(1+\gamma)M^2}{1+\gamma M^2},$$ $$\frac{T_0}{T_0^*} = \frac{(\gamma+1)M^2\left[2+(\gamma-1)M^2\right]}{(1+\gamma M^2)^2}, \qquad \frac{p_0}{p_0^*} = \frac{1+\gamma}{1+\gamma M^2}\left[\frac{2+(\gamma-1)M^2}{\gamma+1}\right]^{\gamma/(\gamma-1)}.$$

For a process from 1 to 2, a ratio is a quotient of starred ratios: \(T_{0,2}/T_{0,1} = (T_0/T_0^*)_2\,/\,(T_0/T_0^*)_1\). The Rayleigh calculator does this for you.

3. The Rayleigh line and thermal choking

Plot every state reachable from a given inlet by adding or removing heat on a temperature–entropy diagram, and you get the Rayleigh line. Since \(ds = \delta q/T\) for reversible heating, adding heat raises entropy. The line has its maximum entropy at M = 1, and the second law tells us which way the state can move:

  • Heating drives a subsonic flow up toward M = 1 (it accelerates, and its pressure falls), and drives a supersonic flow down toward M = 1 (it decelerates, and its pressure rises).
  • Cooling does the reverse, moving the flow away from M = 1.
  • Total pressure always falls when heat is added. The loss is small at low Mach number and large in supersonic flow. Heat added to a fast-moving stream is less useful.
  • On the subsonic branch between \(M = 1/\sqrt{\gamma}\) (0.845 for air) and 1, adding heat actually lowers the static temperature. The flow accelerates so much that the extra kinetic energy exceeds the heat added.

Once the exit reaches M = 1, the flow is thermally choked. The heat added equals the maximum \(c_p T_{0,1}(1/(T_0/T_0^*)_1 - 1)\). Adding more cannot be accommodated with the same inlet state. A subsonic flow responds by reducing its inlet Mach number, so less mass flows. A supersonic flow responds by forming a shock that moves upstream and makes the flow subsonic. This limit is central to the design of any combustor.

4. Fanno flow: adiabatic flow with friction

Now take a constant-area duct with no heat transfer, but with wall shear stress \(\tau_w\). Total temperature is constant. Momentum gains a friction term. With the Darcy–Fanning friction coefficient \(f = \tau_w/(\tfrac12\rho u^2)\) and hydraulic diameter \(D\):

$$dp + \rho u\,du = -\frac{4\tau_w}{D}dx \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{4f\,dx}{D} = \frac{2(1-M^2)}{\gamma M^2\left[1+\frac{\gamma-1}{2}M^2\right]}\frac{dM}{M}.$$

Integrating from M to the sonic point gives the length of duct needed to reach M = 1:

$$\frac{4\bar f L^*}{D} = \frac{1-M^2}{\gamma M^2} + \frac{\gamma+1}{2\gamma}\ln\frac{(\gamma+1)M^2}{2+(\gamma-1)M^2},$$

and the starred ratios

$$\frac{T}{T^*} = \frac{\gamma+1}{2+(\gamma-1)M^2}, \qquad \frac{p}{p^*} = \frac{1}{M}\sqrt{\frac{\gamma+1}{2+(\gamma-1)M^2}}, \qquad \frac{p_0}{p_0^*} = \frac{1}{M}\left[\frac{2+(\gamma-1)M^2}{\gamma+1}\right]^{\frac{\gamma+1}{2(\gamma-1)}}.$$

For a duct of length \(L\) between stations 1 and 2, \(4\bar fL/D = (4\bar fL^*/D)_1 - (4\bar fL^*/D)_2\). For turbulent flow in smooth ducts, \(\bar f \approx 0.0025\)–\(0.005\), only weakly dependent on Reynolds number.

5. The Fanno line and frictional choking

Friction is irreversible, so entropy can only rise. Like heating, it drives subsonic flow up toward M = 1 and supersonic flow down toward M = 1. Unlike heating, it can never be reversed, because there is no "negative friction". The Fanno line on a T–s diagram also has its maximum entropy at M = 1.

The function \(4\bar fL^*/D\) is bounded for supersonic flow: it tends to 0.822 as M → ∞ for γ = 1.4. However high the Mach number, a supersonic flow reaches M = 1 within about 82 diameters divided by 4f̄, which is only a few dozen diameters in practice. If the actual duct is longer than \(L^*\), it is frictionally choked. A subsonic inlet flow then reduces its mass flow. A supersonic one forms a shock inside the duct, and the subsonic flow behind it then chokes at the exit.

In a supersonic flow every arrow except those for total temperature, total pressure and entropy reverses. Shapiro's generalized one-dimensional flow combines all of these drivers, together with mass injection and molecular-weight change, into one influence-coefficient equation:

$$\frac{dM^2}{M^2} = \frac{1+\frac{\gamma-1}{2}M^2}{1-M^2}\left[-2\frac{dA}{A} + (1+\gamma M^2)\frac{dT_0}{T_0} + \gamma M^2\frac{4f\,dx}{D} + \dots\right].$$

The common factor \(1/(1-M^2)\) explains everything in this lesson. The bracket must change sign exactly where M = 1, so to pass smoothly through sonic speed, a nozzle needs a throat, a heated duct needs to switch from heating to cooling, and a duct with friction needs some other driver to oppose it. Real combustors often combine heat addition with a diverging area, which lets more heat be added before the flow chokes.
