Supersonic Wind Tunnels

A supersonic wind tunnel is the nozzle, shock and diffuser theory of Lessons 2–5 built in steel. This lesson covers how such tunnels are laid out and powered, how big their components must be, why they have to be "started", how long a blowdown tunnel can run, why the air must be dried, and how pressures, temperatures, forces and flow patterns are measured.

Section 8.1Why test, and what must be matched

Computation has replaced a great deal of wind-tunnel testing, but not all of it. Turbulence, transition, shock–boundary-layer interaction, separation and unsteady loads are still hard to predict with confidence. Experiments supply the data that validates codes, and the final numbers for anything that will fly.

A test on a scale model reproduces full-scale flight only if the relevant similarity parameters are matched. Dimensional analysis of a compressible, viscous, heat-conducting flow of a perfect gas gives

$$C_F = f\!\left(M_\infty,\ Re_\infty,\ \gamma,\ Pr,\ \frac{T_w}{T_\infty},\ \alpha,\ \text{geometry}\right).$$

In a supersonic tunnel the Mach number is matched exactly, since it is set by the nozzle. γ and Pr match automatically if the test gas is air. The Reynolds number is the hard one. A 1:20 model must see twenty times the flight value of \(\rho V/\mu\) to match \(Re\), and tunnels rarely achieve that. Instead, boundary-layer trips are used to force transition where it would occur in flight, and results are extrapolated in \(Re\). The wall-to-gas temperature ratio matters for heat transfer and boundary-layer behavior. In a cold-flow tunnel the model wall is near the stagnation temperature, which may not match flight.

Section 8.2Anatomy of a supersonic tunnel

Gas flows from left to right through five components:

  1. Settling chamber (stagnation chamber). A large-area, low-speed section where the gas is at its reservoir state \(p_0, T_0\). Honeycomb straightens the flow and fine screens break up large eddies, reducing turbulence before the nozzle.
  2. Nozzle. Converges to the throat and then diverges to the test-section area. Its exit area ratio \(A_e/A^*\) sets the test Mach number. The divergent contour is designed by the method of characteristics so that every expansion wave from the walls is cancelled, delivering uniform, parallel flow. Many tunnels have interchangeable fixed nozzle blocks. Others use flexible steel plates bent by jacks to give any Mach number in a range, or a sliding asymmetric block that changes the throat area.
  3. Test section. Constant area (slightly divergent, in practice, to allow for boundary-layer growth), with optical-quality windows (schlieren glass) and access for model supports and probes.
  4. Diffuser. Decelerates the flow and recovers pressure, so the tunnel needs a smaller pressure ratio to run. A converging section and a second throat slow the supersonic stream before a weak terminal shock, and a diverging subsonic section follows.
  5. Exhaust or return. To atmosphere, to a vacuum tank, or back to a compressor in a closed circuit.

Section 8.3Blowdown, indraft and continuous tunnels

The flow needs a pressure ratio \(p_0/p_b\) across the tunnel. There are three ways to provide it:

TypeHow it worksStrengthsLimitations
Blowdown (pressure–atmosphere or pressure–vacuum)A compressor slowly fills storage tanks to high pressure; a fast-acting control valve then holds the settling-chamber pressure constant while the tanks discharge through the tunnel.Cheap per unit of test-section size; high Reynolds numbers (high p₀); small compressor.Run times of seconds to a minute; T₀ drifts as the tanks expand; heavy, fast pressure regulation needed; large starting loads on models.
Indraft (atmosphere–vacuum)The tunnel draws dried atmospheric air into an evacuated vacuum tank.Constant p₀ and T₀ (the atmosphere); safe; smooth starting.p₀ fixed at 1 atm, so low Reynolds numbers; run time limited by tank volume; drying the intake air is critical.
Continuous (closed circuit)A multistage compressor drives the gas around a closed loop, with coolers to remove the compression heat.Unlimited run time; independent control of p₀ (and so Re) and Mach number; steady conditions.Very expensive; installed power of tens to hundreds of megawatts for large tunnels.

Most university supersonic facilities are blowdown or indraft tunnels. The large continuous tunnels are national facilities, such as the 16-ft supersonic tunnel at Arnold Engineering Development Complex and the Unitary Plan tunnels at NASA Ames and Langley.

Section 8.4Test-section conditions

With isentropic expansion from the reservoir, the test-section state follows from Lesson 1:

$$T = \frac{T_0}{1 + \frac{\gamma-1}{2}M^2}, \qquad p = \frac{p_0}{\left(1 + \frac{\gamma-1}{2}M^2\right)^{\gamma/(\gamma-1)}}, \qquad V = M\sqrt{\gamma RT}, \qquad q = \tfrac12\gamma pM^2.$$

The nozzle throat passes the choked mass flow, which sets throat size and air consumption:

$$\dot m = \frac{p_0A_{t1}}{\sqrt{T_0}}\sqrt{\frac{\gamma}{R}\left(\frac{2}{\gamma+1}\right)^{\frac{\gamma+1}{\gamma-1}}} = 0.0404\,\frac{p_0A_{t1}}{\sqrt{T_0}}\ \ (\text{air, SI}), \qquad A_\text{test} = A_{t1}\,\frac{A}{A^*}\bigg|_{M}.$$

The unit Reynolds number \(\rho V/\mu\) rises in proportion to \(p_0\). It also rises with Mach number at first, because the static temperature, and with it the viscosity, falls. Static temperatures in an unheated tunnel are very low: 129 K (−144 °C) at M = 2.5 from a 290 K reservoir. That has consequences for condensation (Section 8.8) and for models and instrumentation.

Section 8.5Starting: the starting shock and the second throat

A supersonic tunnel does not simply "switch on". Follow the flow as the back pressure is lowered from \(p_0\), exactly as for the converging–diverging nozzle of Lesson 3.

  1. At first the flow is subsonic everywhere. Then the nozzle throat chokes.
  2. A normal shock appears just downstream of the throat and moves down the nozzle as \(p_b\) falls. It grows stronger as it reaches higher Mach numbers.
  3. To start the tunnel, this starting shock must pass right through the test section, at its strongest (Mach \(M_t\)), and be swallowed by the diffuser.

Here the second throat matters. Behind the starting shock, total pressure has dropped to \(p_{0,2}\). The diffuser throat must pass the same mass flow at this lower total pressure. Choked flow gives \(p_{0,1}A_{t1} = p_{0,2}A^*_2\), so the second throat must be at least

$$\boxed{\frac{A_{t2}}{A_{t1}} \ge \frac{p_{0,1}}{p_{0,2}}\bigg|_{M_t}}$$

If it is smaller, the second throat chokes first. The starting shock can then advance only to the point in the nozzle where its Mach number satisfies \(p_{0,1}/p_{0,2} = A_{t2}/A_{t1}\). It stays there however low the back pressure is made, and the tunnel will not start. When the tunnel is started, the flow through the converging diffuser is supersonic, and a much weaker shock stands near the second throat.

This leads to hysteresis. The back pressure needed to start the tunnel (normal shock at \(M_t\)) is lower than the back pressure at which it unstarts (weaker shock at the second-throat Mach number). Once started, the back pressure can be raised again. A variable-geometry diffuser can then close the second throat after starting to improve recovery further. Section 8.6Pressure ratio, power and the diffuser

Without a second throat (a constant-area diffuser with a normal shock at the test Mach number, followed by subsonic diffusion to stagnation), the tunnel needs roughly

$$\frac{p_0}{p_b} \approx \frac{p_{0,1}}{p_{0,2}}\bigg|_{M_t},$$

both to start and to run. With an optimum fixed second throat, the starting requirement is the same, but the running requirement drops to the normal-shock loss at the second-throat Mach number, \(M_{t2}\), the supersonic root of \(A/A^* = A_{t2}/A_{t1}\). Real diffusers do somewhat worse because of boundary layers and shock–boundary-layer interaction. Measured running ratios typically lie between the two curves of Interactive 4.

For a continuous tunnel, the compressor must supply this pressure ratio at the tunnel's mass flow. The ideal (isentropic) compressor power is

$$P = \dot m\,c_pT_0\left[\left(\frac{p_0}{p_b}\right)^{(\gamma-1)/\gamma} - 1\right],$$

so power grows quickly with Mach number. Pressure ratio rises steeply, and so does mass flow per unit test-section area at a given q. That is the main reason high-Mach continuous tunnels are rare and blowdown tunnels are popular.

Section 8.7Run time of a blowdown tunnel

A blowdown tunnel's storage tanks hold a mass \(m_i = p_iV/(RT_i)\). A regulating valve holds \(p_0\) constant while the tank pressure falls. The run ends when the tank pressure drops to a little above \(p_0\), the margin the valve needs. Two limiting models bracket reality:

  • Isothermal tank. Heat-storage material in the tanks, such as steel tubes or plates, keeps the air near its initial temperature. Then \(p \propto m\), and with constant \(T_0\) the mass flow is constant: $$t_\text{run} = \frac{V\,(p_i - p_f)}{R\,T_i\,\dot m}.$$
  • Adiabatic tank. The gas remaining in the tank expands isentropically, so \(p/p_i = (m/m_i)^\gamma\) and \(T/T_i = (m/m_i)^{\gamma-1}\). The supply temperature \(T_0\) falls during the run. Mass flow rises as \(T_0^{-1/2}\), and the run is shorter. The drifting \(T_0\) also changes the test Reynolds number and the static temperature, which matters for condensation.

Section 8.8Condensation and drying the air

Atmospheric air carries water vapor, typically 5–15 grams per kilogram of air. Expanding it to a test-section static temperature of 100–150 K takes the vapor far below its frost point. Condensation does not happen instantly, because nucleation needs time and the nozzle transit takes only milliseconds. The vapor becomes strongly supersaturated, then condenses suddenly in the nozzle. The latent heat released acts like heat addition in a supersonic stream (Rayleigh flow, Lesson 4). It lowers the Mach number, raises the pressure, and can produce visible condensation shocks that make the test-section flow non-uniform.

The cure is to dry the supply air, usually with refrigeration followed by desiccant (silica gel or activated alumina) dryers. Indraft tunnels dry their intake air with a large desiccant bed. A common rule is to keep the specific humidity below about 10⁻⁴ kg/kg (100 ppm by mass) for Mach 2–3 tunnels, a frost point below roughly −40 °C at atmospheric pressure. The amount of supersaturation that can be tolerated depends on the expansion rate, so tunnels are checked by comparing measured and isentropic wall pressures along the nozzle. At higher Mach numbers the static temperature eventually falls toward the saturation temperature of air itself. Unheated air tunnels are therefore limited to roughly Mach 4–5. Beyond that, the supply air must be heated.

Section 8.9Flow quality and calibration

Before a tunnel is used for testing, its empty test section is calibrated:

  • Mach number distribution. A rake of Pitot probes is traversed through the test section. Each reading \(p_{0,2}\), divided by the settling-chamber pressure \(p_{0,1}\), gives the local Mach number through the normal-shock total-pressure ratio. A good tunnel holds M uniform to within about ±0.5% over the test core. Static pressures from wall taps, or from a cone-cylinder static probe on the centerline, give an independent check through \(p/p_0\).
  • Flow angularity. Cone or wedge probes with pressure taps on opposite faces measure flow inclination. Targets are about ±0.1–0.2°.
  • Disturbances. Weak waves from imperfections in the nozzle contour or wall joints show up in schlieren images and Pitot surveys. Turbulent boundary layers on the nozzle walls radiate acoustic noise into the test section, with pressure fluctuations of a percent or so of the static pressure. That noise promotes early boundary-layer transition on models. Specially designed "quiet" tunnels, with polished nozzles that keep laminar wall boundary layers, exist for transition research.
  • Boundary-layer growth. Wall boundary layers thicken along the nozzle and test section, reducing the effective area. Nozzle contours are corrected for displacement thickness, and test sections are often slightly divergent.

The model must also be small enough not to block the tunnel. If its blockage, together with its shock losses and the wall boundary layers, takes up too much of the diffuser's capacity, the tunnel unstarts when the model is inserted. Models are often injected after starting, or tested at angles of attack chosen to keep blockage low. Reflected shocks are a second geometric limit: the model's bow shock must not reflect from the wall back onto the model. Section 8.10Measuring pressure

Pressure is the most common measurement in a supersonic tunnel.

  • Surface static taps. Small holes, typically 0.3–1 mm, drilled normal to the model surface and free of burrs, connected by tubing to transducers. Hole-size errors are small if the hole is a small fraction of the boundary-layer thickness.
  • Pitot and static probes. Pitot probes read p₀₂ (Lesson 2). Static probes are slender cone-cylinders with taps placed where the nose disturbance has decayed.
  • Transducers. Strain-gauge diaphragm transducers, silicon piezoresistive sensors (small, with response to tens of kHz, the workhorse today), capacitive sensors (very accurate and stable, for calibration standards), and piezoelectric gauges (dynamic only, for unsteady pressures and acoustic noise). Electronic pressure scanners multiplex hundreds of taps at high rates, with in-place calibration.
  • Pressure-sensitive paint (PSP). A luminescent coating whose emission is quenched by oxygen. It gives a full-field surface pressure image from a camera, after calibration against taps.

Pneumatic lag. A transducer connected by tubing does not respond instantly. For laminar (Poiseuille) flow in a tube of diameter \(d\) and length \(L\) feeding a transducer volume \(V_t\), the time constant is roughly

$$\tau \approx \frac{128\,\mu\,L\,V_t}{\pi\,d^4\,\bar p}.$$

The fourth power of \(d\) and the inverse dependence on pressure matter a great deal in a blowdown tunnel. Low test-section static pressures and fine tubing can give lags of seconds, a large fraction of the run. Interactive 7 lets you check a tubing layout.

Section 8.11Measuring temperature

A thermometer placed in a high-speed flow reads neither \(T\) nor \(T_0\). The fluid near its surface is slowed by viscosity and heated, but some of that heat conducts away. The recovery temperature is defined through the recovery factor \(r\):

$$T_r = T + r\,\frac{V^2}{2c_p} = T\left(1 + r\,\frac{\gamma-1}{2}M^2\right), \qquad r \approx \sqrt{Pr} \approx 0.85\ (\text{laminar}),\quad r \approx Pr^{1/3} \approx 0.89\ (\text{turbulent}).$$

In a supersonic tunnel the reservoir temperature \(T_0\) is measured in the settling chamber, where the velocity is low, with thermocouples or resistance thermometers. Static temperature is then calculated, not measured. Total-temperature probes for the test section (shielded thermocouples with a vented shroud) are calibrated for their own recovery factor, typically 0.95–0.99. Model surface temperatures, if needed, come from embedded thermocouples, temperature-sensitive paint or infrared thermography.

Section 8.12Measuring forces and moments

Aerodynamic forces are measured with a balance. In supersonic tunnels it is almost always an internal sting balance: a machined metal flexure inside the model, mounted on a sting that enters through the model base. Strain gauges in Wheatstone bridges on the flexure elements respond to normal force, axial force, side force and three moments, so a six-component balance gives the full force and moment vector. Calibration applies known loads in every combination, producing an interaction matrix that corrects for cross-talk between components. Key practical issues are listed below.

  • Starting loads. As the starting shock passes, the model sees brief loads several times the steady test loads. Balances must be sized for these, or the model must be injected after the tunnel starts.
  • Base pressure. The sting alters the base flow. The measured base pressure is subtracted to get "forebody" drag, and sting-interference studies use different sting sizes.
  • Thermal drift. Cold test gas cools the balance during a run, shifting the zero. Temperature compensation and short runs help.
  • Dynamic effects. Model–sting vibration and unsteady separation require filtering and attention to the balance's natural frequency.

Section 8.13Flow visualization: schlieren, shadowgraph and interferometry

The density changes in supersonic flow are large enough to bend light. The refractive index of a gas obeys the Gladstone–Dale relation,

$$n - 1 = K\rho, \qquad K \approx 2.26\times10^{-4}\ \mathrm{m^3/kg}\ \text{(air, visible light)}.$$

A light ray crossing a test section of span \(L\) is deflected by an angle proportional to the density gradient normal to it:

$$\varepsilon_y = \frac{1}{n_\infty}\int_0^L \frac{\partial n}{\partial y}\,dz \approx K L\,\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial y}.$$

Three classic optical systems respond to different derivatives of density:

  • Shadowgraph responds to \(\nabla^2\rho\), the second derivative. Simple: a point light source and a screen. Shocks appear as a bright–dark line pair, slightly displaced from their true position.
  • Schlieren responds to \(\partial\rho/\partial y\), the first derivative normal to a knife edge placed at the focus of the second mirror, which cuts off part of the light source image. Rays bent toward the knife edge are blocked (dark), and rays bent away pass (bright). The orientation of the knife edge selects which gradient component is seen. Sensitivity grows with the focal length of the second mirror and with the fraction of the source image cut off. The usual layout is a Z-type arrangement of two parabolic mirrors. Color schlieren replaces the knife edge with a color filter.
  • Interferometry (e.g. Mach–Zehnder) responds to \(\rho\) itself. Each fringe marks a fixed change in \(\int\rho\,dz\), so densities can be measured quantitatively.

Section 8.14Running a test: a checklist

  1. Plan. Define the test matrix (Mach, Re, α, configurations) and the measurements. Check model size against blockage, starting loads and reflected-shock limits.
  2. Prepare the air. Charge the storage tanks (blowdown) or pump down the vacuum tank (indraft). Check dryer performance and dew point.
  3. Zero and calibrate. Record wind-off zeros of all transducers and the balance, and check calibrations, at the temperature expected during the run if possible.
  4. Start. Open the control valve and bring p₀ up quickly to the set point. Confirm start from the schlieren image and the test-section static pressure.
  5. Acquire. Hold conditions, sweep angle of attack if planned, and record p₀, T₀, all pressures, balance outputs and images. Allow for tubing lag before each data point.
  6. Shut down. Close the valve. Watch for the unstart load and protect the balance.
  7. Reduce data. Compute test-section conditions from p₀, T₀ and the calibrated Mach number. Form coefficients, and apply base-pressure, sting and wall corrections. Estimate uncertainty by propagating the instrument errors.
SafetyHigh-pressure storage, fast-acting valves, vacuum tanks and high-intensity light sources are all hazards. Supersonic facilities run under written procedures with interlocks, pressure-vessel certification and hearing protection.
