Lesson 15 · Part VII

Hypersonic Ground Testing

No wind tunnel can reproduce hypersonic flight. A facility that matches the Mach number usually misses the velocity, the enthalpy and the chemistry. One that matches the enthalpy runs for milliseconds, and one that runs for minutes is either cold or small. Hypersonic testing is therefore the art of choosing which parameters to duplicate for the question being asked. This lesson develops that logic and the physics of the main facility types: heated Mach-6 tunnels, Ludwieg tubes and quiet tunnels such as Purdue's BAM6QT, and shock tubes and reflected-shock tunnels. It closes with the fast instruments that capture heating and instability waves.

Section 15.1Duplication versus simulation

A hypersonic flow is governed by more parameters than the supersonic flows of Lesson 8. Matching them all at once in a ground facility is called duplication. In practice we settle for simulation: matching the parameters that control the phenomenon of interest.

A cold Mach-6 tunnel reaches Mach 6 by expanding air from a reservoir at about 430 K to a static temperature near 53 K. The speed of sound is then only about 145 m/s, so the test velocity is about 870 m/s, less than half the flight velocity at Mach 6 (about 1,800 m/s at 28 km). It matches M and can match Re, but not h₀ or \(T_w/T_0\). Interactive 1 makes the comparison for any flight point and tunnel.

Section 15.2Heating the air: the liquefaction limit

Expanding air to high Mach number cools it enormously: \(T = T_0/(1 + 0.2M^2)\). If the static temperature falls below the saturation temperature at the static pressure, the air's nitrogen and oxygen begin to condense, just as water vapor does in a supersonic tunnel (Lesson 8). Condensation releases heat into the stream and ruins the flow. Hypersonic tunnels must therefore heat their air. Some supersaturation is tolerated in practice, because condensation needs time to nucleate, but the saturation line is the standard design guide. This site uses the saturation curve of nitrogen, which lies close to that of air: ln p is interpolated linearly in 1/T between the triple point (63.15 K, 12.5 kPa), the normal boiling point (77.36 K, 1 atm) and the critical point, with sublimation below the triple point.

Section 15.3Ludwieg tubes and quiet tunnels

A Ludwieg tube is a long, high-pressure tube (the driver) connected through a fast valve or diaphragm to a nozzle, test section and vacuum tank. When the flow starts, an unsteady expansion wave travels up the tube at the speed of sound. Behind it, the gas moves toward the nozzle at a low Mach number \(M_t\), set by the ratio of tube area to throat area, in a uniform state that feeds the nozzle a constant reservoir pressure and temperature. The expansion reflects from the closed upstream end and returns. The steady run lasts until it reaches the nozzle,

$$\Delta t \approx \frac{L}{a_i} + \frac{L}{u + a} \approx \frac{2L}{a_i},$$

after which the cycle repeats at a slightly lower pressure. The reservoir state follows from the unsteady expansion (the Riemann invariant \(u + 2a/(\gamma-1)\) is constant):

$$\frac{a}{a_i} = \frac{1}{1 + \frac{\gamma-1}{2}M_t},\qquad \frac{p}{p_i} = \left(\frac{a}{a_i}\right)^{\frac{2\gamma}{\gamma-1}},\qquad \frac{p_0}{p_i} = \frac{p}{p_i}\left(1 + \frac{\gamma-1}{2}M_t^2\right)^{\frac{\gamma}{\gamma-1}}.$$

Ludwieg tubes are simple and cheap to run, give clean flow without a pressure regulator, and are ideal for quiet tunnels. The Boeing/AFOSR Mach-6 Quiet Tunnel at Purdue is a Ludwieg tube with a heated driver tube, a long polished nozzle that keeps its wall boundary layer laminar, and bleed slots at the throat that remove the incoming boundary layer. Its test-section noise is below about 0.05% of the mean pressure, compared with about 3% in conventional tunnels. Each run consists of several seconds of repeated cycles of about 0.2 s. Section 15.4 gives the details.

Section 15.4The BAM6QT: a user's guide

The Boeing/AFOSR Mach-6 Quiet Tunnel in Purdue's Aerospace Sciences Laboratory was built in the late 1990s and early 2000s under Steven Schneider to give hypersonic transition research a flight-like, low-noise freestream. It first ran quiet at high Reynolds number in 2006. The figures below come from Purdue's published facility papers. Operating limits change as the tunnel is maintained, so check current values with the lab before planning a test.

How the flow is made quiet

  • Throat bleed slots just upstream of the throat remove the contraction-wall boundary layer, which is turbulent, so a fresh laminar boundary layer starts at the throat. The bleed is ducted to the vacuum tank. Closing it gives conventional, noisy flow in the same tunnel, which makes direct quiet/noisy comparisons possible.
  • A long, highly polished nozzle with a gentle curvature delays Görtler and roughness-induced transition of the nozzle-wall boundary layer.
  • Particle filtering of the air, because particles impacting the polished nozzle damage it and trip the flow.
  • Diaphragms downstream of the test section. The burst happens behind the model, so the flow through the nozzle starts without diaphragm fragments or the starting disturbances of an upstream valve.

Planning and running a test

  1. Choose the condition. Pick the unit Reynolds number, then p₀ from it (Interactive 3, or the Ludwieg-tube calculator). Stay below the current quiet limit if you want quiet flow, and decide whether you also need a noisy run for comparison.
  2. Predict the flow on the model. Get the edge conditions on a cone from Taylor–Maccoll (conical-flow calculator). Estimate δ and the second-mode frequency (Lesson 12) to choose sensor bandwidth and sampling rate. Run stability computations (for example, STABL with DPLR mean flow and PSE-Chem) to predict N-factors at the sensor stations.
  3. Check the model will start the tunnel. A large model, a large cone half-angle or a high angle of attack blocks the flow, and the tunnel does not start (Lesson 8). Slender cones are routine. Blunt and large-angle bodies need checking first, and studies of larger cones in the BAM6QT have looked at which half-angles still allow a start.
  4. Prepare the run. Heat the driver to the run temperature and fill it with filtered, dry air. Pump down the tank and the section downstream of the diaphragms. Then arm the data system with a trigger on the burst.
  5. Record the run. The reservoir pressure p₀ and temperature are measured at the upstream end of the nozzle. Use them, together with the isentropic relations at Mach 6, to compute the freestream for each cycle.
  6. Reduce the data. Take power spectral densities of the fast-pressure signals over short windows within a single cycle, where p₀ is nearly constant. Normalize amplitudes by the mean surface pressure. Compare peak frequencies and growth along the model with the stability predictions.

The University of Notre Dame has since built a larger Mach-6 quiet Ludwieg tube on the same principles. Other quiet facilities include the NASA Langley Mach-6 quiet tunnel (now decommissioned) and Mach-6 quiet tunnels in China.

Section 15.5Shock tubes and reflected-shock tunnels

To reach flight enthalpies, the test gas must be heated to thousands of kelvin, which a heater cannot do. A shock tube does it with a shock wave. A high-pressure driver (region 4) is separated from low-pressure test gas (region 1) by a diaphragm. When the diaphragm bursts, a shock runs into the test gas, heating and accelerating it (region 2), while an expansion runs back into the driver. The driver gas behind the expansion (region 3) is separated from the shocked test gas by a contact surface. Matching pressure and velocity across the contact surface gives the shock-tube equation:

$$\frac{p_4}{p_1} = \frac{p_2}{p_1}\left[1 - \frac{(\gamma_4-1)(a_1/a_4)(p_2/p_1 - 1)}{\sqrt{2\gamma_1\left[2\gamma_1 + (\gamma_1+1)(p_2/p_1 - 1)\right]}}\right]^{-2\gamma_4/(\gamma_4-1)}.$$

A light, hot driver (helium or hydrogen, large \(a_4\)) gives a much stronger shock for the same pressure ratio. In a reflected-shock tunnel the incident shock reflects from the end wall, bringing the test gas to rest at very high pressure and temperature (region 5). That gas then expands through a nozzle as the reservoir of a short-duration hypersonic tunnel. Facilities of this kind, including free-piston "Stalker" tunnels (T4 and T5 at Caltech, HEG at DLR) and detonation-driven tunnels, reach stagnation enthalpies of 10–25 MJ/kg for test times of about a millisecond. The reflected shock satisfies

$$\frac{M_R}{M_R^2 - 1} = \frac{M_s}{M_s^2-1}\sqrt{1 + \frac{2(\gamma-1)}{(\gamma+1)^2}(M_s^2-1)\left(\gamma + \frac{1}{M_s^2}\right)}.$$

Section 15.6Expansion tubes, arc jets and ballistic ranges

  • Expansion tubes add a second, low-pressure acceleration tube downstream of the shock tube. The test gas is accelerated by an unsteady expansion rather than stagnated, which avoids dissociating it in a reservoir. They reach orbital and super-orbital velocities for tens to hundreds of microseconds (for example, the X-series tubes at the University of Queensland).
  • Arc-jet facilities heat air with an electric arc and expand it over a material sample for minutes. They duplicate the stagnation enthalpy and heat flux, not the flow field, and they are where heat-shield materials such as PICA and tile coatings are qualified (for example, NASA Ames and JSC arc jets).
  • Ballistic ranges fire a model from a light-gas gun into still or counter-flowing gas. Flight is duplicated around a free-flying model, with photography and radiation measurement, at the cost of very limited instrumentation on the model itself.
  • Flight experiments (HIFiRE, X-43A, X-51A, BOLT, and sounding-rocket transition experiments) remain the final check, which is why ground facilities and CFD are validated against them.

They have a built-in high-pass near 11 kHz, so they measure fluctuations only, not mean pressure. They are the standard second-mode sensor (200–400 kHz on 7° cones in the BAM6QT). Section 15.8Measuring heat flux with surface gauges

A thin-film gauge measures the surface temperature of an insulating substrate. Over a short test the substrate behaves as a semi-infinite solid, and the one-dimensional heat equation links the surface temperature rise to the heat flux. For a constant heat flux q applied at t = 0,

$$\Delta T_s(t) = \frac{2q}{\sqrt\pi}\frac{\sqrt t}{\sqrt{\rho ck}},$$

where \(\sqrt{\rho ck}\) is the thermal effusivity of the substrate. For a measured history \(T_s(t)\), the heat flux follows from the Cook–Felderman integral:

$$q(t) = \frac{\sqrt{\rho ck}}{\sqrt\pi}\int_0^t \frac{dT_s/d\tau}{\sqrt{t - \tau}}\,d\tau.$$

A low-effusivity substrate (polymer, glass ceramic) gives a large, easily measured temperature rise. A high-effusivity one (metal) gives a small rise but disturbs the model's own wall temperature less. Interactive 5 shows the trade.
