Lesson 10 · Part V

Newtonian Theory & Hypersonic Aerodynamics

Newton modeled a fluid as a stream of particles that hit a surface, lose their normal momentum and slide along it. The model fails at low speeds, but in the thin shock layers of hypersonic flow it becomes surprisingly good. This lesson builds the sine-squared law and its modified form, then uses it to find the forces on flat plates, cones, blunted cones and re-entry capsules. It compares the results with exact Taylor–Maccoll cone solutions, and shows how a capsule trims and why its lift points the "wrong" way.

Section 10.1Newton's sine-squared law

Let a uniform stream of particles at speed \(V_\infty\) and density \(\rho_\infty\) strike a flat surface of area A inclined at angle θ to the stream. The mass flux hitting the surface is \(\rho_\infty V_\infty A\sin\theta\). Each particle loses its momentum component normal to the surface, \(V_\infty\sin\theta\), and keeps its tangential component. The normal force is the rate of change of momentum:

$$N = \rho_\infty V_\infty^2 A\sin^2\theta \quad\Rightarrow\quad C_p = \frac{p - p_\infty}{\tfrac12\rho_\infty V_\infty^2} = 2\sin^2\theta.$$

This is Newton's sine-squared law. Surfaces turned away from the flow ("in the shadow") receive no impacts, and Newtonian theory sets \(C_p = 0\) there. Comparing with Lesson 9: in the hypersonic limit with γ → 1, the shock lies on the surface (β → θ) and \(C_{p,2} = 4\sin^2\beta/(\gamma+1) \to 2\sin^2\theta\). Newtonian flow is the double limit \(M_\infty \to \infty\), \(\gamma \to 1\).

Section 10.2Modified Newtonian theory

Lees (1955) observed that the stagnation point of a blunt body does not have \(C_p = 2\). It has the value behind a normal shock, decelerated isentropically to rest: the Rayleigh–Pitot pressure \(p_{0,2}\). Scaling the sine-squared law to match it gives modified Newtonian theory:

$$C_p = C_{p,\max}\sin^2\theta, \qquad C_{p,\max} = \frac{p_{0,2} - p_\infty}{q_\infty} = \frac{2}{\gamma M_\infty^2}\left(\frac{p_{0,2}}{p_\infty} - 1\right).$$

As \(M_\infty \to \infty\), \(C_{p,\max} \to \frac{4}{\gamma+1}\left[\frac{(\gamma+1)^2}{4\gamma}\right]^{\gamma/(\gamma-1)}\), which is 1.839 for γ = 1.4 and approaches 2 as γ → 1. Modified Newtonian theory is accurate on the windward faces of blunt bodies and is the workhorse of conceptual design for capsules and winged re-entry vehicles. It is less accurate on slender bodies and near shoulders, where the flow expands. The Newton–Busemann correction adds the centrifugal force of the gas flowing along a curved surface; in practice it often makes agreement worse for blunt bodies, and plain modified Newtonian is preferred.

Section 10.3The flat plate: lift, drag and L/D

A flat plate at angle of attack α has its lower surface at inclination α and its upper surface in the shadow. With \(C_p = C_{p,\max}\sin^2\alpha\) on the lower surface,

$$C_N = C_{p,\max}\sin^2\alpha, \qquad C_L = C_{p,\max}\sin^2\alpha\cos\alpha, \qquad C_D = C_{p,\max}\sin^3\alpha, \qquad \frac{L}{D} = \cot\alpha.$$

Lift is proportional to \(\alpha^2\) at small angles, not to α as at low speed. \(C_L\) is greatest at α = 54.7° (where \(\tan^2\alpha = 2\)). The inviscid L/D grows without limit as α → 0, but skin friction stops that. With a friction drag coefficient \(C_{D,f}\) (both sides of the plate), L/D has a maximum near \(\alpha \approx (C_{D,f}/C_{p,\max})^{1/3}\cdot 2^{1/3}\), and \((L/D)_{\max}\) falls as friction grows. That is the root of the "L/D barrier" of hypersonic vehicles: well-designed waveriders and CAV configurations reach roughly 4–6 at high Mach number, against 15–20 for subsonic transports.

Section 10.4Cones: Newtonian versus exact

A sharp cone at zero incidence in supersonic flow has an attached conical shock (Lesson 5). Behind it, the flow is not uniform: it keeps turning and compressing isentropically on its way to the surface. The exact inviscid solution satisfies the Taylor–Maccoll equation, an ordinary differential equation in the polar angle θ from the axis, written with velocities scaled by \(V_{\max} = \sqrt{2h_0}\):

$$\frac{\gamma-1}{2}\left[1 - V_r^2 - \left(\frac{dV_r}{d\theta}\right)^2\right]\left[2V_r + \frac{dV_r}{d\theta}\cot\theta + \frac{d^2V_r}{d\theta^2}\right] - \frac{dV_r}{d\theta}\left[V_r\frac{dV_r}{d\theta} + \frac{dV_r}{d\theta}\frac{d^2V_r}{d\theta^2}\right] = 0,$$

with \(V_\theta = dV_r/d\theta\). It is integrated inward from the shock (where the oblique-shock relations give the starting values) until \(V_\theta = 0\), which defines the cone surface. This site solves it with a fourth-order Runge–Kutta scheme and finds the shock angle for a given cone angle by bisection. The results reproduce the NACA 1135 cone charts: for instance, a 10° cone at Mach 2 has β = 31.2°.

The Newtonian estimate for the cone surface is simply \(C_p = C_{p,\max}\sin^2\delta\). Interactive 3 shows that it is poor at low supersonic Mach numbers but approaches the exact result in the hypersonic limit, and is better for cones than for wedges because the three-dimensional relief keeps the shock closer to the surface.

Section 10.5Local surface-inclination methods

Newtonian theory is the simplest of a family of methods that assign each surface panel a pressure from its local inclination alone. Others are:

  • Tangent-wedge: each panel gets the pressure behind an oblique shock at its local inclination. Good for two-dimensional compression surfaces such as inlet ramps and flaps.
  • Tangent-cone: the Taylor–Maccoll surface pressure at the local inclination. Good for slender axisymmetric bodies.
  • Shock-expansion: the leading-edge pressure from an oblique shock, then Prandtl–Meyer expansion along the surface (Lesson 6). Exact for sharp two-dimensional bodies until reflected waves arrive.
  • Prandtl–Meyer or "Newtonian plus Prandtl–Meyer" for leeward, expanding surfaces where Newtonian theory gives zero.

Panel codes built on these (the Hypersonic Arbitrary-Body Program, HABP, and its successors) remain in use for quick configuration studies, trajectory databases and design optimization, where thousands of cases must be run in minutes. Navier–Stokes CFD with real-gas models is reserved for the cases that matter most.

Section 10.6Blunt bodies and capsules at incidence

For a general body, Newtonian theory integrates \(C_p\) over every windward panel. With \(\hat{\mathbf n}\) the outward unit normal and \(\hat{\mathbf V}\) the freestream direction, a panel is windward if \(\hat{\mathbf V}\cdot\hat{\mathbf n} < 0\), and then

$$C_p = C_{p,\max}(\hat{\mathbf V}\cdot\hat{\mathbf n})^2, \qquad \mathbf C_F = -\frac{1}{A_\text{ref}}\oint C_p\,\hat{\mathbf n}\,dA.$$

This site does this on a body of revolution, panelled in 96 azimuthal strips. It reproduces the classic closed forms: \(C_D = 1\) for a sphere (\(C_p = 2\) basis), and \(C_D = 2\sin^2\delta\) for a sharp cone of half-angle δ.

A re-entry capsule like Apollo, Soyuz or Orion has a shallow spherical-cap heat shield (radius \(R_s\) about 1.2 times the diameter for Apollo) and a conical afterbody that sits in the shadow. Something unexpected happens when it is pitched. The force on a nearly flat face acts almost normal to the face, that is, along the capsule's axis. Tilt the face up by α and the axial force tilts with it, so the resultant leans to the side opposite to that of a wing at the same α. The lift of a blunt capsule therefore points opposite to that of a wing at the same angle of attack. Capsules fly with their heat shield tilted (Apollo at α ≈ −20° to −25°) and obtain L/D ≈ 0.3. That small lift was enough to steer Apollo to its splashdown point and to cut the peak deceleration of lunar return to about 7 g, against roughly 15 g for a ballistic entry at the same flight-path angle (try both in Lesson 13).

Section 10.7Trim and static stability

A capsule has no control surfaces: it is trimmed by where its center of gravity sits. Taking moments about the CG,

$$C_{m,cg}(\alpha) = C_{m,\text{nose}}(\alpha) + C_N(\alpha)\frac{x_{cg}}{D} - C_A(\alpha)\frac{z_{cg}}{D}$$

(nose-up moments positive, x measured back from the nose along the axis, z up), and the vehicle flies at the

trim angle where \(C_{m,cg} = 0\). It is statically stable in pitch if a disturbance that raises α produces a restoring, nose-down moment, \(\partial C_{m,cg}/\partial\alpha < 0\). A capsule with its CG on the axis trims at α = 0 and flies ballistically (no lift). Moving the CG off the axis by a few percent of the diameter (the Apollo offset was of order 0.03–0.04 D) makes it trim at α ≈ 20° and generate lift, which the crew or the guidance system pointed by rolling the capsule (bank-angle modulation, Lesson 13).

Static stability also requires the CG to be well forward. For the spherical-cap shield, the pressure resultant on the windward face passes close to the center of the sphere, a distance \(R_s\) behind the nose. A CG between the nose and that point is stable. Many capsule shapes, Apollo's among them, also have a second, unwanted trim point with the apex forward. Afterbody shaping and CG placement must rule it out, since a capsule that flips would enter backward and burn through. Dynamic stability (pitch damping) of capsules is often poor at low supersonic speeds, where an unsteady base flow can drive oscillations. Drogue parachutes are deployed before that regime is reached.

Section 10.8Bluntness and drag

A sharp cone has the least wave drag for its volume, but the heat flux at its tip is unbounded. Blunting the nose of radius \(R_n\) cuts the heating (as \(1/\sqrt{R_n}\), Lesson 12) at a drag cost that Interactive 5 quantifies. For a slender cone, even a modest bluntness raises \(C_D\) noticeably. For a wide-angle cone such as the 70° aeroshells used on Viking, Pathfinder and Mars Science Laboratory, the cone face itself dominates and bluntness hardly matters. Those vehicles want high drag to decelerate in thin atmospheres, so a wide cone is ideal.
