Lesson 13 · Part VI

Atmospheric Entry

Coming home from orbit means getting rid of about 30 MJ of kinetic energy per kilogram of vehicle, more than enough to vaporize it several times over. The atmosphere does the work, but how it does it depends on the trajectory: how steeply the vehicle enters, how much drag and lift it has, and how it steers. This lesson derives the classic ballistic-entry solution of Allen and Eggers, the equilibrium glide of a lifting vehicle, and the entry corridor. It then puts them together in a three-degree-of-freedom trajectory simulator.

Section 13.1The energy problem

At orbital speed, 7.8 km/s, the specific kinetic energy is \(V^2/2\) ≈ 30 MJ/kg; at lunar return, 11 km/s, it is 60 MJ/kg. All of it must go somewhere. Allen and Eggers (NACA, 1953–1957) showed that the fraction that ends up in the vehicle as heat is proportional to the ratio of skin-friction drag to total drag. A slender, low-drag body decelerates mainly by friction and absorbs a large fraction. A blunt, high-drag body puts most of the energy into the shock layer and its wake, which carry it away. This is why every crewed re-entry vehicle has been blunt, and why the 1950s search for a re-entry shape ended with blunt capsules and blunted reentry-vehicle noses.

Hirschel and Weiland's classes face this problem differently. A capsule (RV-NW) decelerates fast and high, with a short, intense heat pulse. A winged orbiter (RV-W) glides for 20–30 minutes at lower heat flux but larger total heat load. A cruise vehicle (CAV) never sheds its energy at all, and must survive steady heating for the whole flight.

Section 13.2Equations of motion

For a vehicle of mass m flying over a spherical, non-rotating planet in a vertical plane, with velocity V, flight-path angle γ (positive above the local horizontal), altitude h, radius r = R + h and bank angle σ (the angle by which the lift vector is rolled away from the vertical), the equations are

$$\frac{dV}{dt} = -\frac{D}{m} - g\sin\gamma,\qquad V\frac{d\gamma}{dt} = \frac{L\cos\sigma}{m} - \left(g - \frac{V^2}{r}\right)\cos\gamma,$$ $$\frac{dh}{dt} = V\sin\gamma,\qquad \frac{ds}{dt} = \frac{R}{r}V\cos\gamma,\qquad D = \frac{\tfrac12\rho V^2}{\beta}\,m,\quad L = \frac{L}{D}D,\quad g = \frac{\mu}{r^2}.$$

The vehicle enters through only two parameters: the ballistic coefficient \(\beta = m/(C_DA)\) and the lift-to-drag ratio L/D. The term \(V^2/r\) is the centrifugal relief that keeps an orbiting body up. Below circular speed, gravity wins and the vehicle must descend unless lift holds it up. The simulator on this page integrates these equations with a fourth-order Runge–Kutta scheme, using the 1976 U.S. Standard Atmosphere extended to 150 km, and Sutton–Graves stagnation heating.

Section 13.3Ballistic entry: Allen and Eggers

For a steep, non-lifting entry, gravity is small compared with drag, and γ stays nearly constant at its entry value \(\gamma_E\) (taken positive downward here). With an exponential atmosphere \(\rho = \rho_0e^{-h/H}\), \(dh = -V\sin\gamma_E\,dt\), and \(dV/dt = -\rho V^2/(2\beta)\):

$$\frac{dV}{V} = \frac{\rho\,dh}{2\beta\sin\gamma_E}\quad\Rightarrow\quad V = V_E\exp\!\left(-\frac{\rho_0H}{2\beta\sin\gamma_E}e^{-h/H}\right).$$

Differentiating the deceleration \(\rho V^2/(2\beta)\) with respect to altitude gives the peak deceleration and where it occurs:

$$a_{\max} = \frac{V_E^2\sin\gamma_E}{2eH}\ \text{ at }\ V = V_Ee^{-1/2} = 0.61V_E,\qquad \rho = \frac{\beta\sin\gamma_E}{H}.$$

Two results stand out. The peak deceleration does not depend on β: a heavy, dense vehicle reaches the same g-load as a light one, only deeper in the atmosphere. And it is set by the entry angle. A 20° entry from orbit gives over 40 g, which is survivable by instruments but not by people. Applying the same analysis to Sutton–Graves heating, \(q \propto \sqrt\rho V^3\), gives peak heating at \(V = V_Ee^{-1/6} = 0.85V_E\) and \(\rho = \beta\sin\gamma_E/(3H)\): earlier and higher than the peak deceleration.

Section 13.4Lifting entry and equilibrium glide

A vehicle with lift can fly a shallow path on which lift, plus centrifugal relief, balances gravity: \(d\gamma/dt \approx 0\) with γ ≈ 0. This equilibrium glide gives

$$\tfrac12\rho V^2\frac{C_LA}{m} = g - \frac{V^2}{r}\quad\Rightarrow\quad \rho_\text{eq}(V) = \frac{2m}{C_LA}\,\frac{g - V^2/r}{V^2},$$

a unique altitude for each velocity. As the vehicle slows, it descends along this curve. Integrating \(ds = V\,dt\) with \(dV/dt = -D/m\) gives the glide range from \(V_0\) to \(V_f\):

$$s = \frac{r}{2}\frac{L}{D}\ln\frac{gr - V_f^2}{gr - V_0^2}.$$

For L/D = 1 from orbital speed this is about 8,000 km. Lift buys cross-range and down-range control, and keeps the deceleration low: in equilibrium glide the g-load is \((g - V^2/r)\sqrt{1 + (L/D)^2}/(L/D)/g_0\), only about 1.5 g at the end for L/D = 1. The Shuttle Orbiter flew close to an equilibrium glide, banking to hold its drag on a planned drag–velocity profile.

Section 13.5The entry corridor and skip-out

Enter too steeply and the deceleration or heating exceeds what the crew or the vehicle can stand. Enter too shallowly at supercircular speed and the vehicle does not lose enough energy on its first pass: it skips back out of the atmosphere, possibly onto an orbit that does not return for a long time. The range of acceptable entry angles is the entry corridor.

For a lifting capsule the corridor is defined by its two extreme lift orientations. The overshoot (shallow) boundary is the shallowest angle at which the vehicle is still captured flying lift down. The undershoot (steep) boundary is the steepest angle at which it stays within the g-limit flying lift up. Between them, the guidance can roll the lift vector to steer. For Apollo lunar returns the corridor was only about 2–3° wide, centered near −6.5°, a narrow target for a spacecraft arriving from 400,000 km away. The simplified model below (constant L/D, no guidance, Sutton–Graves heating) gives a corridor of similar width that opens once the g-limit is about 12 g; with 10 g it closes. That shows how sensitive the corridor is to L/D and to the load limit.

Section 13.6Peak heating versus heat load

A steep entry concentrates the heating into a short, intense pulse; a shallow one spreads it out. The peak heat flux sets the temperature the TPS must survive, and so the choice of material. The integrated heat load \(Q = \int q\,dt\) sets how much of it is needed: ablator thickness, or how deep the heat soaks into an insulator. A trajectory designer trades one against the other. Winged vehicles accept large heat loads at modest fluxes and use reusable radiative TPS. Capsules accept high fluxes for a short time and use ablators.

Section 13.8Steering: bank-angle modulation

A lifting entry vehicle steers by rolling its lift vector about the velocity vector. The vertical component \(L\cos\sigma\) controls the descent rate and so the down-range distance. The horizontal component \(L\sin\sigma\) turns the trajectory sideways, giving cross-range. Because the horizontal component would steadily build up a cross-range error, the vehicle periodically reverses the sign of σ, the roll reversals visible in Shuttle ground tracks. Apollo's guidance flew lift up after entry interface to avoid excessive g, and could perform a controlled "skip" for long ranges. Orion's skip-entry guidance extends this idea, lofting the capsule partly back out of the atmosphere to reach a distant landing site and spread the heat load. Capsules without wings get the same control from their small L/D, which is why a few percent of CG offset (Lesson 10) matters so much.
