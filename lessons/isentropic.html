Section 1.1Why compressible flow is different

In low-speed aerodynamics the density of air is treated as a constant. The flow is then governed by continuity and momentum alone, and pressure and velocity are linked by Bernoulli's equation. Above a few hundred kilometers per hour that picture starts to fail. As the flow accelerates and decelerates around a body, its density changes noticeably, and so does its temperature. Kinetic energy and internal energy trade places. The energy equation, which was decoupled in incompressible flow, becomes as important as the other two.

The results can be dramatic. Shock waves appear, across which pressure can jump by a factor of ten in less than a micrometer. A duct that accelerates a subsonic flow will decelerate a supersonic one. A wing's lift-curve slope rises steeply near Mach 1 and then falls. All of this follows from a handful of thermodynamic ideas, which this lesson develops.

Historical noteThe first systematic theory of compressible flow came from the steam-turbine and artillery problems of the late 19th century. Mach photographed the shock waves around supersonic bullets in 1887. Laval used a converging–diverging nozzle to drive a steam turbine at supersonic jet speed in 1888. Prandtl and his students at Göttingen, Meyer above all, worked out the theory of shocks and expansion waves between 1905 and 1910.

Section 1.2Perfect gases and specific heats

A perfect gas is one in which intermolecular forces are negligible except during collisions. Air at the pressures and temperatures of most aerodynamic flows behaves this way to within a fraction of a percent, and obeys

$$p = \rho R T, \qquad R = \frac{\mathcal{R}}{\mathcal{M}}, \qquad R_\text{air} = \frac{8314.5}{28.97} = 287\ \mathrm{J\,kg^{-1}\,K^{-1}}.$$

The specific internal energy \(e\) and enthalpy \(h = e + p/\rho\) of a perfect gas depend on temperature alone. The specific heats are their temperature derivatives:

$$c_v = \left(\frac{\partial e}{\partial T}\right)_v, \qquad c_p = \left(\frac{\partial h}{\partial T}\right)_p, \qquad c_p - c_v = R, \qquad \gamma \equiv \frac{c_p}{c_v}.$$

A gas whose specific heats vary with temperature is thermally perfect. If they are also constant, it is calorically perfect, and then

$$e = c_vT, \qquad h = c_pT, \qquad c_p = \frac{\gamma R}{\gamma - 1}, \qquad c_v = \frac{R}{\gamma - 1}.$$

Kinetic theory explains the value γ = 1.4. A diatomic molecule such as N₂ or O₂ stores energy in three translational and two rotational modes, each holding \(\tfrac12 kT\), so \(c_v = \tfrac52R\) and \(\gamma = 7/5\). A monatomic gas such as helium has only the three translational modes, so \(\gamma = 5/3\). At higher temperatures the molecules begin to vibrate. Each vibrational mode, once fully excited, adds another \(R\) to \(c_v\), and γ falls toward 9/7 ≈ 1.29. Interactive 1 shows this happening in air, using the harmonic-oscillator model for N₂ (characteristic vibrational temperature θv = 3390 K) and O₂ (θv = 2270 K):

$$\frac{c_{v,\text{vib}}}{R} = \frac{(\theta_v/T)^2\,e^{\theta_v/T}}{\left(e^{\theta_v/T}-1\right)^2}.$$

Section 1.3The first law

For a fixed mass of gas that receives heat \(\delta q\) and does work \(\delta w\) on its surroundings, per unit mass,

$$de = \delta q - \delta w.$$

For a reversible process the only work is boundary work, \(\delta w = p\,dv\) with \(v = 1/\rho\). Heat and work are path-dependent, but \(e\) is a state variable, so \(de\) depends only on the end states. Writing the first law in terms of enthalpy gives the companion form \(dh = \delta q + v\,dp\).

Section 1.4Entropy and the second law

The first law allows a process to run either way. Experience says it cannot: heat flows from hot to cold, and a shock compresses but never expands. The second law captures this through entropy \(s\), defined by

$$ds = \frac{\delta q_\text{rev}}{T}, \qquad ds = \frac{\delta q}{T} + ds_\text{irrev}, \qquad ds_\text{irrev} \ge 0.$$

For an adiabatic process, then, \(ds \ge 0\). Entropy can only stay constant (reversible) or increase (irreversible). In a gas flow the irreversibilities are viscous dissipation, heat conduction across temperature gradients, mass diffusion, and the intense versions of all three inside shock waves. Combining the two laws for a reversible process gives the Gibbs relations, \(T\,ds = de + p\,dv = dh - v\,dp\). Since these relate state variables only, they hold for any process. Integrating them for a calorically perfect gas:

$$s_2 - s_1 = c_p\ln\frac{T_2}{T_1} - R\ln\frac{p_2}{p_1} = c_v\ln\frac{T_2}{T_1} - R\ln\frac{\rho_1}{\rho_2}.$$

The entropy change depends only on the end states, not on how the gas got from one to the other. Interactive 2 places two states on a temperature–entropy diagram, where lines of constant pressure (isobars) are exponential curves.

Section 1.5Isentropic relations

A process that is both adiabatic and reversible is isentropic. Setting \(s_2 = s_1\) in the entropy equation gives the isentropic relations, used in nearly every calculation that follows:

$$\frac{p_2}{p_1} = \left(\frac{\rho_2}{\rho_1}\right)^{\gamma} = \left(\frac{T_2}{T_1}\right)^{\gamma/(\gamma-1)}.$$

They apply to much more of a real flow than might be expected. Outside thin boundary layers, viscosity and conduction are negligible. Shocks, where entropy is produced, are thin surfaces whose effect is captured by jump conditions (Lesson 2). Everywhere else the flow is adiabatic and reversible, which is why these relations dominate high-speed aerodynamics.

Section 1.6Compressibility

The compressibility τ of a fluid is its fractional volume change per unit pressure change:

$$\tau = -\frac{1}{v}\frac{\partial v}{\partial p} = \frac{1}{\rho}\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial p} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad d\rho = \rho\,\tau\,dp.$$

It depends on the process. τT holds temperature fixed, and τs holds entropy fixed, which is the relevant one for flows. For water, τT ≈ 5×10⁻¹⁰ m²/N. For air at 1 atm, τT = 1/p ≈ 10⁻⁵ m²/N, about 20 000 times larger.

Whether that matters depends on the pressure changes the flow creates. These are of order the dynamic pressure, \(dp \sim \tfrac12\rho V^2\), so \(d\rho/\rho \sim \tfrac12\rho\tau_sV^2\). Since \(\rho\tau_s = 1/a^2\), this is \(\tfrac12 M^2\). The exact isentropic result from rest is

$$\frac{\rho}{\rho_0} = \left(1 + \frac{\gamma-1}{2}M^2\right)^{-1/(\gamma-1)} \approx 1 - \frac{M^2}{2} + \dots$$

This is the source of the rule of thumb: below M ≈ 0.3 the density changes by less than about 5%, and the flow can be treated as incompressible.

Section 1.7The governing equations

For an inviscid, non-conducting flow with no body forces, mass, momentum and energy conservation on a fixed control volume \(\mathcal V\) bounded by \(S\) read:

$$\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\iiint_\mathcal{V} \rho\, d\mathcal{V} + \oiint_S \rho\mathbf{V}\cdot d\mathbf{S} = 0$$ $$\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\iiint_\mathcal{V} \rho\mathbf{V}\, d\mathcal{V} + \oiint_S (\rho\mathbf{V}\cdot d\mathbf{S})\mathbf{V} = -\oiint_S p\, d\mathbf{S}$$ $$\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\iiint_\mathcal{V} \rho\left(e + \tfrac{V^2}{2}\right) d\mathcal{V} + \oiint_S \rho\left(e + \tfrac{V^2}{2}\right)\mathbf{V}\cdot d\mathbf{S} = \iiint_\mathcal{V}\dot q\rho\, d\mathcal{V} - \oiint_S p\mathbf{V}\cdot d\mathbf{S}$$

Applying them to an infinitesimal volume gives the differential (Euler) equations:

$$\frac{D\rho}{Dt} + \rho\nabla\cdot\mathbf V = 0, \qquad \rho\frac{D\mathbf V}{Dt} = -\nabla p, \qquad \rho\frac{D}{Dt}\left(h + \frac{V^2}{2}\right) = \frac{\partial p}{\partial t} + \rho\dot q.$$

With \(p = \rho RT\) and \(h = c_pT\) these form a closed set. For steady, adiabatic flow, the energy equation integrates along a streamline to a very powerful result:

$$h + \frac{V^2}{2} = h_0 = \text{const}, \qquad c_pT + \frac{V^2}{2} = c_pT_0.$$

If every streamline starts in the same uniform freestream, \(h_0\) is the same everywhere in the flow. This holds even through shocks, which are adiabatic though not isentropic.

Section 1.8The speed of sound

Consider a weak pressure wave moving into still gas. In a frame moving with the wave, gas enters at speed \(a\) with state \(p,\rho\) and leaves at \(a + da\) with \(p + dp,\ \rho + d\rho\). Continuity and momentum across it give

$$\rho a = (\rho + d\rho)(a + da), \qquad p + \rho a^2 = (p + dp) + (\rho + d\rho)(a + da)^2 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad a^2 = \frac{dp}{d\rho}.$$

The wave is weak and fast, so no heat crosses it and the process is isentropic. Using the isentropic relation \(p \propto \rho^\gamma\):

$$a = \sqrt{\left(\frac{\partial p}{\partial\rho}\right)_s} = \sqrt{\frac{\gamma p}{\rho}} = \sqrt{\gamma RT} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\rho\,\tau_s}}.$$

For a perfect gas \(a\) depends on temperature (and gas) alone, not on pressure. Newton originally assumed an isothermal process and got \(\sqrt{RT}\), 16% too low, until Laplace corrected him in 1816. Kinetic theory adds that \(a\) is about 74% of the molecules' mean speed, \(\bar c = \sqrt{8RT/\pi}\). The Mach number \(M = V/a\) compares directed motion with random molecular motion:

$$\frac{V^2/2}{e} = \frac{V^2/2}{c_vT} = \frac{\gamma(\gamma-1)}{2}M^2.$$

Section 1.9Total (stagnation) conditions

Imagine a fluid element at a point where the static values are \(p,\ T,\ \rho\) and the velocity is \(V\). Now slow it to rest.

Total temperature. If the deceleration is adiabatic, the energy equation gives the temperature it reaches:

$$c_pT_0 = c_pT + \frac{V^2}{2} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{T_0}{T} = 1 + \frac{\gamma-1}{2}M^2.$$

Total pressure and density. If the deceleration is also reversible, that is isentropic, the pressure and density reached are \(p_0\) and \(\rho_0\):

$$\frac{p_0}{p} = \left(1 + \frac{\gamma-1}{2}M^2\right)^{\frac{\gamma}{\gamma-1}}, \qquad \frac{\rho_0}{\rho} = \left(1 + \frac{\gamma-1}{2}M^2\right)^{\frac{1}{\gamma-1}}.$$

These are point properties. Every point in any flow has values of \(T_0, p_0, \rho_0\), defined by an imaginary process, whether or not the flow actually stagnates there. In a steady adiabatic flow, \(T_0\) (and \(h_0\)) is the same on every streamline from a uniform freestream. If the flow is also isentropic, \(p_0\) and \(\rho_0\) are constant too. If it is adiabatic but not isentropic, for example across a shock or in a boundary layer, \(p_0\) falls, and the fall measures the entropy produced:

$$s_2 - s_1 = -R\ln\frac{p_{0,2}}{p_{0,1}}.$$

Why Bernoulli fails. Bernoulli's equation assumes constant density. Its "total pressure" \(p + \tfrac12\rho V^2\) is only the first two terms of the series

$$\frac{p_0}{p} = 1 + \frac{\gamma}{2}M^2\left(1 + \frac{M^2}{4} + \frac{2-\gamma}{24}M^4 + \dots\right).$$

The error is about 2% at M = 0.3, 6% at M = 0.5, and very large in supersonic flow. Interactive 5 makes the comparison for any state you choose.

Section 1.10Sonic reference conditions and M*

A second reference state is the one the element would reach if accelerated or decelerated adiabatically to exactly M = 1. Setting M = 1 in the total relations gives

$$\frac{T^*}{T_0} = \frac{2}{\gamma+1}, \qquad \frac{p^*}{p_0} = \left(\frac{2}{\gamma+1}\right)^{\frac{\gamma}{\gamma-1}}, \qquad \frac{\rho^*}{\rho_0} = \left(\frac{2}{\gamma+1}\right)^{\frac{1}{\gamma-1}}.$$

For γ = 1.4 these are 0.8333, 0.5283 and 0.6339. The value p*/p₀ = 0.528 recurs constantly: it is the pressure ratio at which a converging nozzle chokes. Dividing the energy equation by \(a^2/(\gamma-1)\) gives several useful forms,

$$\frac{a^2}{\gamma-1} + \frac{V^2}{2} = \frac{a_0^2}{\gamma-1} = \frac{\gamma+1}{2(\gamma-1)}a^{*2} = \frac{V_\text{max}^2}{2}.$$

The last is the speed the gas would reach expanding to absolute zero, \(V_\text{max} = a_0\sqrt{2/(\gamma-1)}\), which is 2.236 a₀ for air. The characteristic Mach number \(M^* = V/a^*\) is related to M by

$$M^{*2} = \frac{(\gamma+1)M^2}{2 + (\gamma-1)M^2}.$$

M* equals 1 when M does and tends to \(\sqrt{(\gamma+1)/(\gamma-1)} = 2.449\) as M → ∞. Because it stays bounded, it is convenient in shock theory: the Prandtl relation for a normal shock is simply \(M_1^*M_2^* = 1\).

Worked examples

At a point in an airflow the static pressure is \(p\), the static temperature \(T\) and the velocity \(V\). Find the total temperature and total pressure, and compare with Bernoulli's equation.

Discussion

T₀ needs only an adiabatic deceleration; p₀ needs an isentropic one. At this Mach number, Bernoulli's "total pressure" is far too low, because density cannot be treated as constant.

A Pitot tube on an aircraft reads \(p_0\). The static port reads \(p\), and the outside air temperature is \(T\). The flow is subsonic. Find the true airspeed, then compare with the incompressible Bernoulli formula.

Discussion

The compressible formula follows from inverting p₀/p for M. Bernoulli overestimates the velocity because it overestimates the dynamic pressure needed to produce p₀ − p. Airspeed indicators are calibrated with the compressible formula at sea-level density, which is the definition of "calibrated airspeed".

Air at rest in a large reservoir at \(p_0, T_0\) expands isentropically through a nozzle to the static pressure \(p\). Find the temperature, Mach number and velocity at that point.

Discussion

The static temperature drops well below freezing even from a room-temperature reservoir. Moisture in the supply air would condense or freeze here, which is why supersonic wind tunnels dry their air (Lesson 8).
