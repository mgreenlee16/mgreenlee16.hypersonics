Lesson 9 · Part V

What Makes a Flow Hypersonic

Hypersonic flow is not simply "supersonic, but faster". Above roughly Mach 5, physical effects that are small at supersonic speeds start to dominate: shock layers become thin, nose bluntness leaves a thick entropy layer, boundary layers grow so fast that they reshape the inviscid flow, and the gas becomes hot enough to vibrate, dissociate and ionize. This lesson maps where hypersonic vehicles fly and develops the features that set the regime apart.

Section 9.1When is a flow hypersonic?

The usual rule of thumb is \(M_\infty > 5\), but there is no sharp boundary. Anderson's definition is the useful one: a flow is hypersonic when some or all of the following become important as the Mach number rises.

  • Thin shock layers. The shock lies close to the body, so the region between them is thin. This is what makes Newtonian theory (Lesson 10) work.
  • Entropy layers. Every real vehicle has a blunt nose. The strongly curved bow shock produces a layer of high-entropy, low-velocity gas that wraps the body and is "swallowed" by the boundary layer downstream.
  • Viscous interaction. Laminar boundary-layer thickness grows like \(M_\infty^2/\sqrt{Re_x}\). At high Mach numbers the boundary layer displaces the outer flow enough to change the surface pressure, skin friction and heating.
  • High-temperature effects. The kinetic energy of a vehicle at 7 km/s, turned into heat in the shock layer, excites molecular vibration and dissociates O₂ and N₂. The gas no longer has a constant γ (Lesson 11).
  • Low-density effects. At very high altitude the mean free path becomes comparable with the vehicle size, and the continuum equations fail.

Hirschel and Weiland add a design-oriented view. What matters for a real vehicle is the thermal state of the surface, meaning its temperature and heat flux, together with which flow phenomena (transition, separation, shock interactions, real-gas effects) govern the loads. The following lessons follow that thread from shocks to heating, entry trajectories, propulsion and ground testing.

Section 9.3The hypersonic limit of the shock relations

Write the oblique-shock relations with \(M_1\sin\beta\) large. The terms with 1 in them drop out, and the ratios approach limits that no longer depend on the Mach number in the same way:

$$\frac{p_2}{p_1} \to \frac{2\gamma}{\gamma+1}M_1^2\sin^2\beta, \qquad \frac{\rho_2}{\rho_1} \to \frac{\gamma+1}{\gamma-1}, \qquad \frac{T_2}{T_1} \to \frac{2\gamma(\gamma-1)}{(\gamma+1)^2}M_1^2\sin^2\beta.$$

The density ratio saturates at 6 for γ = 1.4. However strong the shock, the gas can only be squeezed six-fold, while pressure and temperature keep rising with \(M^2\). In terms of the pressure coefficient, \(C_p = (p_2-p_1)/q_1\),

$$C_{p,2} \to \frac{4}{\gamma+1}\sin^2\beta.$$

For a slender wedge (small θ and β), \(\tan\theta \approx \theta\), and the θ–β–M relation in the limit \(M_1 \to \infty\) gives

$$\frac{\beta}{\theta} \to \frac{\gamma+1}{2} = 1.2 \quad (\gamma = 1.4).$$

The shock angle is only 20% larger than the wedge angle. Interactive 2 compares the exact relations with these limits as the Mach number rises.

Section 9.4Thin shock layers

Because \(\rho_2/\rho_1\) is large, mass conservation needs only a thin layer to carry the flow that has crossed the shock. On a wedge in the limit, β − θ ≈ θ(γ − 1)/2, only 0.2θ for γ = 1.4. As γ → 1, which is roughly what happens in a dissociating gas, the shock collapses onto the surface. The flow then behaves as though the gas particles hit the body and slide along it. That picture is Newton's 1687 model of fluid resistance, which fails badly at low speeds but becomes accurate in the hypersonic limit. Lesson 10 develops it.

Thin shock layers have practical consequences:

  • The shock can lie inside, or interact with, the boundary layer near the leading edge (viscous interaction).
  • On an inlet ramp, the shock moves close to the cowl lip. That is why a scramjet forebody is long and slender (Lesson 14).
  • Bow shocks from a vehicle's nose can strike wings, fins or cowls, producing shock–shock interactions with intense local heating (Edney's types I–VI).

Section 9.5Hypersonic similarity and Mach-number independence

For slender bodies at high Mach number, the small-disturbance equations (hypersonic small-disturbance theory, HSD) show that the flow depends on \(M_\infty\) and the thickness ratio τ only through their product, the hypersonic similarity parameter

$$K = M_\infty\tau.$$

Two affinely related slender bodies (same shape, scaled in thickness) at the same K and γ have the same \(C_p/\tau^2\), \(C_L/\tau^2\) and \(C_D/\tau^3\). For a wedge of half-angle θ, HSD gives closed forms:

$$\frac{\beta}{\theta} = \frac{\gamma+1}{4} + \sqrt{\left(\frac{\gamma+1}{4}\right)^2 + \frac{1}{K^2}}, \qquad \frac{C_p}{\theta^2} = \frac{\gamma+1}{2} + \sqrt{\left(\frac{\gamma+1}{2}\right)^2 + \frac{4}{K^2}}.$$

As \(K\to\infty\), both become constants: \(C_p/\theta^2 \to \gamma+1\). This is Mach-number independence. Above some Mach number, pressure coefficients, shock shapes and force coefficients stop changing. It also holds for blunt bodies, where \(C_{p,\max}\) approaches 1.839 for γ = 1.4. Mach-number independence is why a Mach 6–10 wind tunnel can predict the inviscid aerodynamics of a Mach 20 vehicle, provided high-temperature and viscous effects are handled separately.

Section 9.6Blunt bodies and the entropy layer

Sharp noses cannot survive hypersonic heating: Lesson 12 shows stagnation-point heat flux scales as \(1/\sqrt{R_n}\), so every real vehicle is blunted. The bow shock ahead of a blunt nose is normal on the axis and weakens toward the Mach angle far away. Each streamline crosses it at a different strength, and so gains a different entropy:

$$\frac{\Delta s}{R} = -\ln\frac{p_{0,2}}{p_{0,1}}\bigg|_{M_n = M_\infty\sin\beta}.$$

Streamlines near the axis have passed the nearly normal part of the shock. They carry high entropy, low total pressure and low velocity, and form a thin entropy layer hugging the body that persists far downstream. The boundary layer grows into it and eventually "swallows" it. Until then, the entropy layer changes the boundary-layer edge conditions, which matters for heating and transition on blunted cones.

Billig's correlations (1967) describe the shock shape for a sphere-cone and a cylinder-wedge of nose radius R:

$$\text{sphere: }\frac{\Delta}{R} = 0.143\,e^{3.24/M_\infty^2},\quad \frac{R_c}{R} = 1.143\,e^{0.54/(M_\infty-1)^{1.2}};\qquad \text{cylinder: }\frac{\Delta}{R} = 0.386\,e^{4.67/M_\infty^2},\quad \frac{R_c}{R} = 1.386\,e^{1.8/(M_\infty-1)^{0.75}},$$ $$x = R + \Delta - R_c\cot^2\mu\left[\sqrt{1 + \frac{y^2\tan^2\mu}{R_c^2}} - 1\right],$$

where Δ is the stand-off distance, \(R_c\) the shock radius of curvature at the axis, μ the freestream Mach angle, and x is measured upstream from the center of the nose.

Section 9.7Viscous interaction

On a flat plate the laminar boundary-layer thickness at hypersonic speed scales as

$$\frac{\delta}{x} \propto \frac{M_\infty^2}{\sqrt{Re_x}}.$$

The \(M^2\) comes from the temperature rise inside the boundary layer: viscous dissipation heats the gas to near the recovery temperature, its density falls, and the layer thickens to carry the same mass. A thick boundary layer acts like a thickened body. The outer flow sees it as a wedge, generating an induced shock and raising the surface pressure. The governing parameter is the viscous interaction parameter

$$\bar\chi = \frac{M_\infty^3\sqrt{C}}{\sqrt{Re_x}}, \qquad C = \frac{\rho_w\mu_w}{\rho_\infty\mu_\infty}\ \ (\text{Chapman–Rubesin}).$$

Large \(\bar\chi\) (near the leading edge, at high Mach number, low Reynolds number) is strong interaction. Small \(\bar\chi\) is weak interaction. For an insulated plate with γ = 1.4, the induced pressure is approximately

$$\text{weak: }\ \frac{p}{p_\infty} = 1 + 0.31\bar\chi + 0.05\bar\chi^2, \qquad \text{strong: }\ \frac{p}{p_\infty} = 0.514\bar\chi + 0.759.$$

Viscous interaction raises skin friction and heating near leading edges and changes control-surface effectiveness. Hirschel and Weiland also stress that a vehicle's windward surface sees "weak" interaction over most of its length but strong interaction at leading edges and on small, low-Reynolds-number models, which matters when scaling wind-tunnel data to flight.

Section 9.8High temperature and low density

Stagnation temperatures in hypersonic flight are enormous: \(T_0 = T_\infty(1 + 0.2M_\infty^2)\) gives about 18,000 K for a calorically perfect gas at Mach 25. Real air never gets that hot. The energy goes into vibration, dissociation and ionization instead, as Lesson 11 shows, and the post-shock temperature at orbital speed is closer to 6,000–7,000 K. The perfect-gas relations of Lessons 1–8 still describe the cold freestream and most wind-tunnel flows, but not the shock layer of a re-entering vehicle.

At high altitude the Knudsen number \(Kn = \lambda/L\) grows, where λ is the mean free path. Approximate regimes:

KnRegimeMethod
< 0.01ContinuumEuler / Navier–Stokes
0.01–0.1Slip flowNavier–Stokes with velocity-slip and temperature-jump boundary conditions
0.1–10TransitionalDirect Simulation Monte Carlo (DSMC)
> 10Free molecularKinetic theory of individual molecular impacts

Kinetic theory links Kn to the familiar parameters: \(Kn \approx 1.26\sqrt{\gamma}\,M_\infty/Re_\infty\). High Mach number with low Reynolds number, at high altitude or on small models, means rarefaction.

Worked examples
