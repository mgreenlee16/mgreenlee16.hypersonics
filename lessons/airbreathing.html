Lesson 14 · Part VI

Hypersonic Air-Breathing Propulsion

A rocket carries its oxidizer; an air-breathing engine takes it from the atmosphere. In principle that is a large advantage: a hydrogen scramjet can have a specific impulse several times that of the best rocket. But above Mach 5 the air arrives so hot and fast that the classic ramjet stops working, and combustion must happen in a supersonic stream that passes through the engine in about a millisecond. This lesson analyzes ramjets and scramjets as Brayton cycles with component efficiencies (after Heiser and Pratt), compares engines across the Mach range, and looks at inlets, the flight corridor and commercial applications.

Section 14.1Why breathe air?

Specific impulse \(I_{sp}\) is thrust per unit weight flow of propellant carried. A hydrogen–oxygen rocket reaches about 450 s in vacuum (the RS-25 is 452 s), a kerosene–oxygen rocket about 350 s. An air-breathing engine carries only fuel, and the fuel is a small fraction of the mass flow through the engine (about 3% by mass for stoichiometric hydrogen–air). Its \(I_{sp}\) can be in the thousands of seconds. The price is that the engine only works inside the atmosphere, within a narrow band of dynamic pressure, and its thrust falls as the vehicle approaches orbital speed. That makes air-breathing propulsion attractive for long-range hypersonic cruise (Hirschel and Weiland's CAV class), and as the lower stages of reusable launch systems.

Section 14.2Thrust, specific impulse and efficiency

For an engine with air mass flow \(\dot m_0\), fuel-to-air ratio f, flight speed \(V_0\) and exhaust speed \(V_{10}\) expanded to ambient pressure,

$$F = \dot m_0\left[(1+f)V_{10} - V_0\right],\qquad I_{sp} = \frac{F}{\dot m_f g_0} = \frac{F/\dot m_0}{fg_0},\qquad \eta_o = \frac{FV_0}{\dot m_fh_{PR}},$$

where \(h_{PR}\) is the fuel's heating value (120 MJ/kg for hydrogen, about 43–44 MJ/kg for hydrocarbons) and \(\eta_o\) the overall efficiency, the fraction of fuel energy turned into propulsive work. For cruise, the Breguet range equation combines propulsion and aerodynamics:

$$R = \eta_o\frac{h_{PR}}{g_0}\frac{L}{D}\ln\frac{m_i}{m_f}.$$

A hypersonic cruiser needs high \(\eta_o\) and high L/D at the same time, which is hard. L/D falls as Mach number rises (Lesson 10), and the engine's \(\eta_o\) rises with Mach number up to a point. The product \(\eta_o L/D\) is the figure of merit.

Section 14.3Ramjets and why they give way to scramjets

A ramjet has no compressor. Its inlet slows the air, through oblique shocks and a terminal normal shock, to a low subsonic Mach number. That raises the static temperature and pressure toward their stagnation values. Fuel then burns at low speed, and the hot gas expands through a nozzle. Ramjets work well from about Mach 2 to 5. Above that, three problems grow quickly.

  • Temperature. Slowing the air to M ≈ 0.3 recovers nearly the total temperature, \(T_t = T_0(1 + 0.2M_0^2)\): about 3,000 K at Mach 8. Air at that temperature is already partly dissociated, so burning fuel in it mostly breaks molecules apart instead of raising the temperature. The energy comes back only if the gas recombines in the nozzle, which it does not fully do.
  • Pressure. The static pressure in a subsonic combustor approaches the total pressure, which at Mach 8 is hundreds of times ambient, stressing the structure.
  • Losses. The terminal normal shock at a high Mach number destroys total pressure.

The answer is to slow the air only partly, keep it supersonic through the combustor (typically \(M_3\) ≈ 2–4), and burn fuel in the supersonic stream: the supersonic-combustion ramjet, or scramjet. Section 14.4The scramjet as a Brayton cycle

Heiser and Pratt analyze the scramjet as a thermodynamic Brayton cycle with four stations: freestream (0), burner entry (3), burner exit (4) and nozzle exit (10). The inlet is described by its cycle static temperature ratio \(\psi = T_3/T_0\) and a compression efficiency \(\eta_c\). The burner adds heat at constant pressure with efficiency \(\eta_b\), and the nozzle expands to ambient pressure with efficiency \(\eta_e\):

$$V_3^2 = V_0^2 - 2c_{p}(T_3 - T_0),\qquad \frac{p_3}{p_0} = \left[\frac{\psi}{\psi(1-\eta_c) + \eta_c}\right]^{c_p/R},$$ $$(1+f)\left(c_{pb}T_4 + \tfrac12V_4^2\right) = c_pT_3 + \tfrac12V_3^2 + \eta_bfh_{PR},\qquad V_4 = \frac{V_3}{1+f},$$ $$T_{10} = T_4\left\{1 - \eta_e\left[1 - \left(\frac{p_0}{p_3}\right)^{R/c_{pb}}\right]\right\},\qquad V_{10}^2 = V_4^2 + 2c_{pb}(T_4 - T_{10}).$$

Here fuel is injected with no axial momentum, and \(c_p\) is taken for air (γ = 1.4) in the inlet and for hot products (γ = 1.3) in the burner and nozzle. The model is a ramjet if ψ is large enough to make \(M_3 < 1\), and a scramjet otherwise. The same equations cover both. Constant specific heats overestimate \(T_4\), because real combustion products dissociate, so the cycle values are optimistic above about 2,500 K.

Section 14.6The flight corridor

An air-breathing vehicle flies where the air is dense enough for its engine but thin enough for its structure. Heiser and Pratt bound the corridor by dynamic pressure, typically 24–96 kPa (500–2,000 lbf/ft²). The lower bound is set by combustion: burner pressure must stay above roughly half an atmosphere for reliable ignition and reaction rates. The upper bound comes from structural loads and heating. On a constant-q trajectory, altitude rises with Mach number, and the total temperature, which drives heating, rises steeply. Interactive 4 shows the corridor with a few reference flights.

Section 14.7Inlets and forebody compression

A scramjet inlet compresses the air through a series of oblique shocks, usually starting on the vehicle's own forebody, so that the airframe is part of the engine. Its quality is measured by the total-pressure recovery \(p_{0,3}/p_{0,0}\), or better by the kinetic-energy efficiency: the fraction of the freestream kinetic energy the compressed flow would recover if expanded isentropically back to \(p_0\),

$$\eta_{KE} = \frac{V_x^2}{V_0^2},\qquad V_x^2 = V_3^2 + 2c_pT_3\left[1 - \left(\frac{p_0}{p_3}\right)^{R/c_p}\right].$$

Values of 0.95–0.98 are typical of good hypersonic inlets. Because shock losses grow with shock strength, the same compression is cheaper in several weak shocks than in one strong one (Lesson 5). Hirschel and Weiland stress the viscous side of forebody design: the boundary layer that grows along a long forebody enters the inlet. It must not separate in the inlet's adverse pressure gradient, which is why vehicles such as the X-43A tripped their forebody boundary layers to make them turbulent before the inlet. Its thickness also reduces the effective capture area.

Section 14.8Commercial and access-to-space applications

Hypersonic air-breathing propulsion has flown only in short experiments: the X-43A (about 10 s of scramjet operation per flight) and the X-51A (about 4–6 minutes of hydrocarbon scramjet operation, 2010–2013), along with programs in Australia (HyShot, HIFiRE), Russia and China. Turning it into commercial capability means solving several coupled problems:

  • Point-to-point transport. Breguet's equation favors high \(\eta_oL/D\). At Mach 5–6 with hydrocarbon fuel, studies show transoceanic ranges are possible, but at Mach 8+ hydrogen is needed. Its low density (≈ 71 kg/m³ as a liquid) forces large fuel volumes, which hurt L/D. Heating, sonic boom, airport compatibility and cost per seat remain open questions.
  • Access to space. Air-breathing first stages could reduce oxidizer mass, but a scramjet cannot start below about Mach 4 and loses thrust near Mach 12–15. Practical systems combine cycles. TBCC (turbine-based combined cycle) pairs a turbojet with a ramjet/scramjet. RBCC (rocket-based) embeds a rocket in the scramjet duct. Precooled engines (such as Reaction Engines' SABRE concept) chill the incoming air so that a compressor can work to about Mach 5. Mode transition between cycles, and the drag of carrying several engines, are central difficulties.
  • Thermal management. Combustor walls see heat fluxes of megawatts per square meter. The fuel itself, hydrogen or endothermic hydrocarbons that absorb heat by cracking, is the main heat sink.
  • Ground testing. No facility reproduces the full flight enthalpy, pressure, size and test time at once (Lesson 15), so engine development relies on combinations of tunnels, CFD and flight experiments.
