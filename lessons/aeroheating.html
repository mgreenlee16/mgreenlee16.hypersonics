Lesson 12 · Part V

Hypersonic Boundary Layers & Aeroheating

At hypersonic speed the hardest design problem is usually not lift or drag but heat. Drag grows like \(V^2\); convective heating at the nose grows like \(V^3\). This lesson computes boundary-layer heating with the reference-temperature method, the stagnation-point heat flux of a blunt nose, and the radiation-adiabatic wall temperature that a hot structure reaches. It then turns to laminar–turbulent transition, including the second-mode instability measured in quiet tunnels, and to how thermal protection systems cope.

Section 12.1The thermal state of the surface

Hirschel and Weiland build their treatment of hypersonic design around the thermal state of the surface: the wall temperature \(T_w\), the heat flux into the wall \(q_w\), and their gradients. It matters in two ways. The first is thermal loads: the structure and the thermal protection system (TPS) must survive \(T_w\) and absorb or reject \(q_w\) and the time-integrated heat load \(Q\). The second is viscous thermal surface effects: \(T_w\) feeds back on the flow. A hot wall thickens the boundary layer, delays or promotes transition, enlarges separation at flaps and inlet ramps, and changes skin friction. Wind tunnels with cold models get these effects wrong unless they are corrected for.

Most hypersonic vehicles that fly for more than a few seconds are radiation cooled. The surface heats up until it radiates away almost all the heat that convection brings in. Its temperature is then the radiation-adiabatic temperature of Section 12.5, which may be 1,000–2,000 K.

Section 12.2Compressible boundary layers and the Reynolds analogy

Inside a hypersonic boundary layer, the kinetic energy of the edge flow is dissipated into heat. An insulated wall reaches the adiabatic wall (recovery) temperature

$$T_{aw} = T_e\left(1 + r\frac{\gamma-1}{2}M_e^2\right),\qquad r \approx \sqrt{Pr}\ \text{(laminar)},\quad r \approx Pr^{1/3}\ \text{(turbulent)},$$

a little below the total temperature. A wall cooler than \(T_{aw}\) receives heat at a rate set by the Stanton number, and the Reynolds analogy links heat transfer to skin friction:

$$q_w = \rho_eu_ec_p(T_{aw} - T_w)\,St, \qquad St \approx \frac{C_f}{2}Pr^{-2/3}.$$

Heat transfer and skin friction rise and fall together. That is why turbulent boundary layers, with three to five times the skin friction of laminar ones at the same station, also heat three to five times as much.

Section 12.3The reference-temperature method

The density and viscosity vary strongly through a hypersonic boundary layer. Eckert's reference-temperature method evaluates the incompressible skin-friction formulas at a single representative temperature:

$$\frac{T^*}{T_e} = 1 + 0.032M_e^2 + 0.58\left(\frac{T_w}{T_e} - 1\right),\qquad \rho^* = \frac{p_e}{RT^*},\quad \mu^* = \mu(T^*),\quad Re_x^* = \frac{\rho^*u_ex}{\mu^*},$$ $$C_f^* = \frac{0.664}{\sqrt{Re_x^*}}\ \text{(laminar)},\qquad C_f^* = \frac{0.0592}{(Re_x^*)^{0.2}}\ \text{(turbulent)},\qquad q_w = \rho^*u_ec_p(T_{aw} - T_w)\frac{C_f^*}{2}Pr^{-2/3}.$$

On a cone, the boundary layer is thinner because the surface area grows downstream, spreading the boundary-layer fluid. The Mangler transformation relates the two: a laminar cone has \(\sqrt3\) times the skin friction and heating of a flat plate at the same local Reynolds number. The turbulent factor is about 1.15. The method is accurate to roughly 10–20% for attached flow, and it is what Interactive 1 uses. The boundary-layer edge state comes from an oblique shock (windward plate at incidence) or from the Taylor–Maccoll solution (cone).

Section 12.4Stagnation-point heating

The highest convective heat flux on a blunt body is at its stagnation point. Fay and Riddell (1958) solved the boundary layer there for dissociated air in equilibrium:

$$q_w = 0.763\,Pr^{-0.6}(\rho_e\mu_e)^{0.4}(\rho_w\mu_w)^{0.1}\sqrt{\left(\frac{du_e}{dx}\right)_s}\,(h_{0e} - h_w)\left[1 + (Le^{0.52} - 1)\frac{h_D}{h_{0e}}\right],$$

where \(h_D\) is the dissociation energy carried by the edge gas and Le the Lewis number. Newtonian theory gives the velocity gradient, \((du_e/dx)_s = \frac{1}{R_n}\sqrt{2(p_e - p_\infty)/\rho_e}\), and so \(q_w \propto 1/\sqrt{R_n}\). Correlating many such solutions for cold walls gives the simple and widely used Sutton–Graves formula:

$$q_s = k\sqrt{\frac{\rho_\infty}{R_n}}\,V_\infty^3,\qquad k = 1.7415\times10^{-4}\ \text{kg}^{1/2}/\text{m}\ \text{for Earth's air (SI, W/m}^2).$$

Three conclusions follow. Heating grows with the cube of speed. It grows only with the square root of density, so it peaks earlier and higher in an entry than the deceleration does (Lesson 13). And it falls with nose radius, which is why re-entry vehicles have large, blunt noses: Allen and Eggers' 1950s insight that ended the search for slender, pointed re-entry warheads. For a hot wall, multiply by \((1 - h_w/h_0)\).

Section 12.5The radiation-adiabatic wall

A radiation-cooled surface reaches a steady temperature when the heat convected in equals the heat radiated out (conduction into the structure is small for a good insulator):

$$q_w(T_w) = \varepsilon\sigma T_w^4.$$

Hirschel calls the solution the radiation-adiabatic temperature \(T_{ra}\). With emissivity ε ≈ 0.8–0.9 (the black coatings of Shuttle tiles and carbon–carbon), it is far below the recovery temperature. The flow could heat the wall to 6,000 K, but radiation holds it near 1,500 K. Because \(q_w\) itself falls as \(T_w\) rises (through \(h_0 - h_w\)), the solution is found by iteration. Section 12.6Laminar–turbulent transition

Whether the boundary layer is laminar or turbulent changes heating by a factor of three to five, so transition is a first-order design driver. It is also one of the least predictable phenomena in hypersonics. A vehicle designed for turbulent heating carries a heavier TPS than one that can count on laminar flow. Transition starts when small disturbances (freestream noise, roughness, vibration) enter the boundary layer through receptivity and grow as instability waves. The main paths are:

  • First mode: a compressible version of the Tollmien–Schlichting wave. It is most unstable as oblique waves and is weakened by wall cooling.
  • Second mode (Mack mode): acoustic waves trapped between the wall and the relative-sonic line inside the boundary layer. It dominates above about Mach 4 on sharp cones and flat plates. Its wavelength is about twice the boundary-layer thickness, so its frequency is high (often 100–500 kHz in tunnels). Wall cooling destabilizes it.
  • Crossflow on swept leading edges and on cones at angle of attack, and the Görtler instability on concave surfaces such as flares and inlet ramps.
  • Roughness and bypass paths: tile gaps, steps and protruding gap fillers can trip the flow directly. Shuttle flight experience used correlations in the parameter \(Re_\theta/M_e\) to predict roughness-induced transition.
  • Entropy-layer effects on blunted cones: nose bluntness moves transition downstream at first, then, at large bluntness, transition reverses and moves forward again.

Conventional wind tunnels radiate acoustic noise from their turbulent nozzle-wall boundary layers, at levels of about 1% of the static pressure. That forces transition much earlier than in flight. Quiet tunnels keep the nozzle-wall boundary layer laminar, using suction at the throat, polished nozzles and very clean air, and reach noise levels below about 0.05%, comparable to flight. The Boeing/AFOSR Mach-6 Quiet Tunnel (BAM6QT) at Purdue is the best known. Measurements there of second-mode waves on cones, with fast pressure sensors on the model, have been central to understanding hypersonic transition (Lesson 15 covers the facility).

Section 12.7Second-mode waves on a cone

Interactive 4 uses a simple but useful estimate: the second mode's wavelength is about twice the boundary-layer thickness, \(\lambda \approx 2\delta\), and it travels at nearly the edge velocity, so \(f \approx u_e/(2\delta)\). The boundary-layer thickness comes from the Blasius solution in Howarth–Dorodnitsyn variables, with a Crocco–Busemann temperature profile and the Chapman–Rubesin factor at Eckert's reference temperature, divided by √3 for the cone. For the BAM6QT case the estimate gives roughly 300 kHz near x = 0.35 m. That is close to the frequencies measured on 7° cones in that tunnel, and it explains why the instrumentation needs megahertz-bandwidth pressure sensors. Two trends follow from \(\delta \propto \sqrt{x/Re}\): the frequency falls along the cone as \(x^{-1/2}\), and it rises with unit Reynolds number as \(\sqrt{Re}\). Use the estimate for orientation only. Linear stability theory (for example, the PSE-Chem code in the STABL suite) is needed for growth rates and N-factors.

Section 12.8Thermal protection systems

ApproachHow it worksExamples
Heat sinkA thick metal skin absorbs the heat load in its heat capacityEarly ICBM nose cones (copper), Mercury test flights (beryllium)
AblationA charring material pyrolyzes, blowing gas that blocks convective heating, and is carried away; one useApollo (Avcoat), Orion, Stardust and Dragon (PICA-family), RV nose tips (carbon phenolic)
Radiative, insulatingA low-conductivity surface reaches Tra and re-radiates; insulation protects the structureShuttle silica tiles (HRSI, LRSI) and blankets
Hot structureLoad-carrying structure of high-temperature material operating at TraShuttle nose cap and wing leading edges (reinforced carbon–carbon), X-37B; CMC flaps (X-38)
Active coolingCoolant (often the fuel) flows through the structure, or is transpired through itScramjet combustor walls cooled by hydrogen or hydrocarbon fuel (X-51A)

Two other phenomena drive TPS design. Surface catalysis: atoms produced by dissociation recombine at a catalytic wall and release their dissociation energy into it. A non-catalytic coating (such as the Shuttle tiles' borosilicate glass) can reduce stagnation heating noticeably compared with a fully catalytic metal. Shock interactions can be worse still. When a bow shock from one component meets another component's shock, an impinging supersonic jet can strike the surface (Edney's type IV interaction) and multiply the local heating several times over. In 1967 an X-15A-2 flight to Mach 6.7 carried a dummy ramjet on a pylon, and shock impingement burned through the pylon.

Section 12.9Wall temperature during an entry

Heating changes along a trajectory. A thin metal skin of thickness d, density ρ and specific heat c, insulated on its back face, follows

$$\rho c\,d\,\frac{dT_w}{dt} = q_{cw}(t)\left(1 - \frac{h_w}{h_0}\right) - \varepsilon\sigma T_w^4.$$

A thick skin (heat sink) heats slowly and never reaches the radiation-adiabatic temperature: it soaks up the heat load. A thin skin follows \(T_{ra}(t)\) closely. Interactive 5 integrates this equation along a trajectory computed by the entry simulator of Lesson 13.
