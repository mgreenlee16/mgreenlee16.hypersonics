Normal and Moving Shocks

When a supersonic flow has to slow down, it often does so abruptly, in a shock wave a fraction of a micrometer thick. Across a normal shock the flow becomes subsonic, pressure, density and temperature jump, and stagnation pressure is lost. This lesson derives the jump relations, explains the trends you must know for Part I, and shows how to handle a shock that moves, such as a blast wave.

Section 8.1Why shocks form

Small disturbances (pressure signals) travel through a gas at the speed of sound a = √(γRT), about the speed of the molecules themselves. In subsonic flow, signals from a body travel upstream ahead of it, and the oncoming gas adjusts gradually: the streamlines start to turn well before the body. In supersonic flow (M > 1) the gas moves faster than the signals, so they cannot propagate upstream; the flow has no warning, and the adjustment happens suddenly, in a shock.

A source moving at M > 1 sends out sound waves that pile up on a Mach cone of half-angle \(\mu = \sin^{-1}(1/M)\). Outside the cone the gas is undisturbed ("zone of silence"); inside, it has felt the body. A strong disturbance, such as a blunt body or a wedge, produces a shock, which is oblique when it is inclined to the flow and normal when perpendicular to it (in front of a blunt body near the axis, in a duct, or in a Pitot probe's bow shock).

Section 8.2What a shock is

A shock is a very thin region of non-equilibrium flow, only a few mean free paths thick (≈ 0.1–0.3 μm at sea-level density), across which the gas is rapidly compressed and decelerated. Inside it, viscosity and heat conduction are huge; outside, the flow is inviscid. On the scale of any vehicle it is a discontinuity. From state 1 (upstream) to state 2 (downstream): pressure, temperature and density increase; velocity and Mach number decrease. The flow through the shock is adiabatic but not isentropic.

Section 8.3The jump conditions

Put a thin control volume around a stationary normal shock in steady, 1-D flow. With no area change, heat or work, the conservation laws plus a calorically perfect gas give:

$$\begin{aligned}\text{mass:}&\quad \rho_1u_1 = \rho_2u_2\\ \text{momentum:}&\quad p_1 + \rho_1u_1^2 = p_2 + \rho_2u_2^2\\ \text{energy:}&\quad h_1 + \tfrac12u_1^2 = h_2 + \tfrac12u_2^2\ \ (h_{01} = h_{02})\\ \text{state:}&\quad p_2 = \rho_2RT_2,\qquad h_2 = c_pT_2\end{aligned}$$

With the upstream state known, that is five equations in five unknowns (ρ₂, u₂, p₂, T₂, h₂). The mass and momentum relations go back to Stokes (1848); the energy relation to Rankine (1870) and Hugoniot (1887).

Section 8.4The normal-shock relations

Outline of the solution

Divide momentum by mass flux: \(u_1 - u_2 = p_2/(\rho_2u_2) - p_1/(\rho_1u_1) = a_2^2/(\gamma u_2) - a_1^2/(\gamma u_1)\). The energy equation in terms of the sonic speed a* (Lesson 7), \(a^2 = \frac{\gamma+1}{2}a^{*2} - \frac{\gamma-1}{2}u^2\), turns this into the Prandtl relation

$$u_1u_2 = a^{*2}\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad M_1^*M_2^* = 1 .$$

A supersonic upstream flow (M₁* > 1) must therefore be subsonic downstream. Converting from M* to M gives the relations below.

$$M_2^2 = \frac{1 + \frac{\gamma - 1}{2}M_1^2}{\gamma M_1^2 - \frac{\gamma - 1}{2}},\qquad \frac{\rho_2}{\rho_1} = \frac{u_1}{u_2} = \frac{(\gamma + 1)M_1^2}{2 + (\gamma - 1)M_1^2},$$ $$\frac{p_2}{p_1} = 1 + \frac{2\gamma}{\gamma + 1}\left(M_1^2 - 1\right),\qquad \frac{T_2}{T_1} = \frac{p_2}{p_1}\,\frac{\rho_1}{\rho_2},\qquad T_{02} = T_{01}.$$

Everything depends on the upstream Mach number M₁ (and γ). As M₁ → 1 the changes vanish: the shock degenerates into a sound wave. As M₁ → ∞ (γ = 1.4): \(M_2 \to \sqrt{(\gamma-1)/(2\gamma)} = 0.378\), \(\rho_2/\rho_1 \to (\gamma+1)/(\gamma-1) = 6\), while p₂/p₁ and T₂/T₁ grow without bound. The normal-shock table (Appendix B) lists M₂, p₂/p₁, ρ₂/ρ₁, T₂/T₁, p₀₂/p₀₁ and p₀₂/p₁ against M₁.

Section 8.5Entropy and stagnation-pressure loss

With the jump ratios, Lesson 6's entropy equation gives

$$s_2 - s_1 = c_p\ln\frac{T_2}{T_1} - R\ln\frac{p_2}{p_1} = c_p\ln\!\left[\left(1 + \tfrac{2\gamma}{\gamma+1}(M_1^2 - 1)\right)\frac{2 + (\gamma-1)M_1^2}{(\gamma+1)M_1^2}\right] - R\ln\!\left[1 + \tfrac{2\gamma}{\gamma+1}(M_1^2 - 1)\right].$$

For M₁ > 1 this is positive, as the second law requires. For M₁ < 1 the same formulas give s₂ < s₁ and ρ₂ < ρ₁: an "expansion shock", which is impossible. Normal shocks exist only for supersonic upstream flow.

Imagine bringing the gas to rest isentropically on each side. T₀ is the same (adiabatic), so the entropy equation between the two stagnation states reduces to

$$s_2 - s_1 = -R\ln\frac{p_{02}}{p_{01}}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{p_{02}}{p_{01}} = e^{-(s_2 - s_1)/R} < 1 .$$

A shock always loses stagnation pressure: weakly near M₁ = 1 (p₀₂/p₀₁ = 0.9989 at M₁ = 1.1), heavily at high Mach number (0.3283 at M₁ = 3). This is why supersonic inlets and diffusers try to decelerate through weak oblique shocks rather than one normal shock.

Section 8.7Stagnation conditions behind a shock

A common problem has three states: (1) the supersonic flow upstream, (2) just behind the normal shock, (3) the gas brought to rest isentropically behind it (the nose of a blunt body, or the mouth of a Pitot probe). Then

$$p_3 = p_{02} = p_1\cdot\frac{p_{02}}{p_1}\ \text{or}\ p_{01}\cdot\frac{p_{02}}{p_{01}},\qquad T_3 = T_{02} = T_{01} = T_1\left(1 + \tfrac{\gamma-1}{2}M_1^2\right).$$

Two traps: p₀₂ is not p₂ (p₂ is the static pressure; p₀₂ is p₂ raised isentropically from M₂), and T₀ does not change across the shock. Use the isentropic table for state 1 → 01, the shock table for 1 → 2, and the isentropic table again (at M₂) for 2 → 02, or the p₀₂/p₁ column directly.

Section 8.8Moving shocks: changing the frame

A shock moving at speed W into still gas (a blast wave, the shock in a shock tube, a piston pushed into a tube) looks steady to an observer riding with it. In that shock frame the gas arrives at u₁ = W and leaves at u₂; the normal-shock relations apply with

$$M_1 = \frac{W}{a_1} \quad(\text{the shock Mach number } M_s).$$

Back in the lab frame the gas behind the shock moves in the direction of the shock at

$$u_p = W - u_2 = W\left(1 - \frac{\rho_1}{\rho_2}\right),\qquad u_2 = M_2a_2 .$$

Static properties (p, T, ρ, a, s) are the same in every frame; anything built from the velocity (M, T₀, p₀, h₀) is not. For example, in the lab frame the still gas ahead has T₀ = T₁, while the gas behind has T₀ = T₂ + up²/(2cp), so T₀ increases across a moving shock (the shock does work on the gas), unlike the stationary case.

Section 8.9Key equations

Normal shock (γ = 1.4 numbers)

$$M_2^2 = \frac{1 + \frac{\gamma-1}2M_1^2}{\gamma M_1^2 - \frac{\gamma-1}2},\qquad \frac{p_2}{p_1} = 1 + \frac{2\gamma}{\gamma+1}(M_1^2 - 1)$$ $$\frac{\rho_2}{\rho_1} = \frac{u_1}{u_2} = \frac{(\gamma+1)M_1^2}{2 + (\gamma-1)M_1^2},\qquad \frac{T_2}{T_1} = \frac{p_2/p_1}{\rho_2/\rho_1}$$ $$T_{02} = T_{01},\qquad s_2 - s_1 = -R\ln\frac{p_{02}}{p_{01}}$$

Moving shock and Mach cone

$$M_s = \frac{W}{a_1},\qquad u_p = W - M_2a_2 = W(1 - \rho_1/\rho_2)$$ $$\mu = \sin^{-1}\frac1M,\qquad u_1u_2 = a^{*2}$$

Limits M₁ → ∞: M₂ → 0.378, ρ₂/ρ₁ → 6.

Section 8.10Worked examples

Worked examples

Air at M₁ = 2.5, p₁ = 40 kPa, T₁ = 250 K passes through a normal shock. Find M₂, p₂, T₂, u₁ and u₂.

Solution

Table (M₁ = 2.50): M₂ = 0.5130, p₂/p₁ = 7.125, ρ₂/ρ₁ = 3.333, T₂/T₁ = 2.1375, p₀₂/p₀₁ = 0.4990. p₂ = 285.0 kPa, T₂ = 534.4 K. u₁ = 2.5√(1.4·287·250) = 792.3 m/s; u₂ = u₁/3.333 = 237.7 m/s (also M₂√(γRT₂) = 0.5130·463.4 = 237.7 ✓).

For Ex 8.1, find p₀₁, p₀₂, T₀₂ and s₂ − s₁.

Solution

p₀₁ = 40(17.09) = 683.4 kPa (isentropic table, p₀/p at M = 2.5). p₀₂ = 0.4990(683.4) = 341.0 kPa (or p₁·p₀₂/p₁ = 40·8.526). T₀₂ = T₀₁ = 250(2.25) = 562.5 K. Δs = −287 ln 0.4990 = 199.5 J/(kg K). Half the stagnation pressure is lost.

A blast wave moves at 600 m/s into still air at 290 K and 100 kPa. Find the pressure and temperature behind it and the velocity of the air it sets in motion.

Solution

a₁ = 341.4 m/s, Ms = 1.758. Shock frame: p₂/p₁ = 3.438, T₂/T₁ = 1.500, M₂ = 0.6263 (exact values at M = 1.758; the closest table entry, 1.76, gives nearly the same). p₂ = 343.8 kPa, T₂ = 435.1 K, a₂ = 418.1 m/s, u₂ = M₂a₂ = 261.8 m/s. Lab frame: up = 600 − 261.8 = 338.2 m/s toward the direction the shock travels, M = 338.2/418.1 = 0.809.

T₀ in the shock frame: 290 + 600²/2009 = 469.2 K on both sides. In the lab frame: 290 K ahead and 435.1 + 338.2²/2009 = 492.0 K behind.

A bullet flies at 850 m/s through air at 288 K. Find the Mach angle.

Solution

a = 340.2 m/s, M = 2.499, μ = sin⁻¹(1/2.499) = 23.6°.

Section 8.11Check your understanding

Explain in one word why T₀ does not change across a stationary normal shock.

Answer

Adiabatic.

Is the flow through a shock isentropic? Why can't you use p₀ = constant across it?

Answer

No. Inside the shock, viscosity and heat conduction are large and irreversible, so entropy rises and p₀ falls: p₀₂/p₀₁ = e−Δs/R.

What do the shock relations predict for M₁ = 0.8, and why is that rejected?

Answer

An expansion (ρ₂ < ρ₁, p₂ < p₁) with s₂ < s₁, which violates the second law for an adiabatic process.

For a moving shock, which of these differ between the lab and shock frames: p₂, T₂, M₂, T₀₂, u₂?

Answer

M₂, T₀₂ and u₂ differ; p₂ and T₂ do not.
