General Loading: the Fourier Solution

Elliptic loading is the ideal, but real wings are tapered, twisted or rectangular because they are cheaper to build or easier to make safe at stall. To analyze them we write the circulation as a Fourier sine series, which turns Prandtl's integro-differential equation into a set of linear equations, and gives the lift and induced drag in two short formulas.

Section 2.1The Fourier sine series for Γ

Change the spanwise variable from \(y\) to an angle θ:

$$y = -\frac b2\cos\theta,\qquad y_0 = -\frac b2\cos\theta_0,\qquad 0 \le \theta \le \pi .$$

θ = 0 is the left tip, θ = π/2 the root and θ = π the right tip. Since Γ must vanish at both tips, a sine series is the natural expansion:

$$\Gamma(\theta) = 2bV_\infty\sum_{n=1}^{N}A_n\sin n\theta .$$

Each term vanishes at θ = 0 and π. The factor \(2bV_\infty\) makes the coefficients \(A_n\) dimensionless. For the elliptic distribution, \(\sqrt{1-(2y/b)^2} = \sin\theta\), so \(\Gamma = \Gamma_0\sin\theta\): only the first term appears, with

$$A_1 = \frac{\Gamma_0}{2bV_\infty},\qquad A_n = 0\ \ (n \ge 2).$$

The higher harmonics are the departures from elliptic. Odd harmonics (n = 3, 5, …) are symmetric about the root; even ones are antisymmetric (they would describe, e.g., a rolling wing or a deflected aileron).

Section 2.2Substituting into Prandtl's equation

Derivation

Differentiate the series and change variables with \(dy/d\theta = \tfrac b2\sin\theta\):

$$\frac{d\Gamma}{d\theta} = 2bV_\infty\sum nA_n\cos n\theta\quad\Rightarrow\quad \frac{d\Gamma}{dy}\,dy = \frac{d\Gamma}{d\theta}\,d\theta = 2bV_\infty\sum nA_n\cos n\theta\,d\theta .$$

With \(y_0 - y = \tfrac b2(\cos\theta - \cos\theta_0)\), the induced angle becomes

$$\alpha_i(\theta_0) = \frac{1}{4\pi V_\infty}\int_0^\pi\frac{2bV_\infty\sum nA_n\cos n\theta}{\tfrac b2(\cos\theta - \cos\theta_0)}\,d\theta = \frac1\pi\sum nA_n\int_0^\pi\frac{\cos n\theta\,d\theta}{\cos\theta - \cos\theta_0}.$$

Glauert's integral, \(\int_0^\pi \frac{\cos n\theta}{\cos\theta - \cos\theta_0}d\theta = \pi\frac{\sin n\theta_0}{\sin\theta_0}\), evaluates it:

$$\alpha_i(\theta_0) = \sum_{n=1}^N nA_n\frac{\sin n\theta_0}{\sin\theta_0}.$$

The effective-angle term is \(\Gamma/(\pi V_\infty c) = \frac{2b}{\pi c}\sum A_n\sin n\theta_0\). Adding the two:

$$\boxed{\;\sum_{n=1}^{N}A_n\sin n\theta_0\left(\frac{2b}{\pi c(\theta_0)} + \frac{n}{\sin\theta_0}\right) = \alpha(\theta_0) - \alpha_{L=0}(\theta_0)\;}$$

This is the monoplane equation. For a section slope \(a_0\) other than 2π, replace \(2b/(\pi c)\) by \(4b/(a_0c)\). The integral is gone: the equation is algebraic and linear in the unknown \(A_n\).

Section 2.3Solving: collocation and symmetry

Truncate the series to N terms and enforce the equation at N stations θ₀₁ … θ₀N. That gives N linear equations for N unknowns, \(\mathbf M\mathbf A = \boldsymbol\alpha - \boldsymbol\alpha_{L=0}\), with

$$M_{mn} = \sin n\theta_{0m}\left(\frac{2b}{\pi c(\theta_{0m})} + \frac{n}{\sin\theta_{0m}}\right).$$

Skip the tips (θ₀ = 0, π), where \(\sin\theta_0 = 0\). For a symmetric wing at zero sideslip, the loading is symmetric about the root, all even \(A_n\) vanish, and it is enough to use odd n = 1, 3, 5, … and stations on one half of the span, e.g. θ₀ = π/8, π/4, 3π/8, π/2 for four terms. The solution converges quickly; four to ten terms give CL and δ to engineering accuracy.

Section 2.4Lift coefficient

$$C_L = \frac{2}{V_\infty S}\int_{-b/2}^{b/2}\Gamma\,dy = \frac{2}{V_\infty S}\int_0^\pi 2bV_\infty\sum A_n\sin n\theta\;\frac b2\sin\theta\,d\theta = \frac{2b^2}{S}\sum A_n\int_0^\pi\sin n\theta\sin\theta\,d\theta .$$

The sines are orthogonal: \(\int_0^\pi\sin n\theta\sin m\theta\,d\theta = \pi/2\) if m = n and 0 otherwise. Only n = 1 survives:

$$\boxed{\;C_L = \pi\,AR\,A_1\;}$$

The total lift depends on \(A_1\) alone. The other harmonics move lift around the span without changing the total.

Section 2.5Induced angle, induced drag, δ and e

With \(\alpha_i(\theta) = \sum mA_m\sin m\theta/\sin\theta\) and \(\Gamma\,dy = 2bV_\infty\sum A_n\sin n\theta\,\tfrac b2\sin\theta\,d\theta\):

$$C_{D_i} = \frac{2}{V_\infty S}\int\Gamma\alpha_i\,dy = \frac{2b^2}{S}\sum_n\sum_m mA_nA_m\int_0^\pi\sin n\theta\sin m\theta\,d\theta = \pi AR\sum_{n=1}^N nA_n^2 .$$

Factor out \(A_1^2\) and use \(A_1 = C_L/(\pi AR)\):

$$C_{D_i} = \pi AR A_1^2\left[1 + \sum_{n\ge2}n\left(\frac{A_n}{A_1}\right)^2\right] = \frac{C_L^2}{\pi AR}(1 + \delta) = \frac{C_L^2}{\pi e AR},$$ $$\delta = \sum_{n=2}^N n\left(\frac{A_n}{A_1}\right)^2 \ \ (\text{induced drag factor}),\qquad e = \frac1{1+\delta}\ \ (\text{span efficiency factor}).$$

Since δ is a sum of squares, δ ≥ 0 and e ≤ 1, with equality only for elliptic loading: for a given span and lift, the elliptic distribution minimizes the induced drag. The drag polar of the wing is \(C_D = C_{D,0} + C_L^2/(\pi eAR)\): profile (skin friction plus form) drag plus induced drag, or zero-lift drag plus drag due to lift.

The general lift slope is written in the same spirit as \(a = a_0/[1 + (a_0/\pi AR)(1 + \tau)]\), where τ is another small factor found from the \(A_n\). Unlike δ, τ is not negligible (it is about 0.1–0.25 for rectangular wings).

Section 2.6Example: the rectangular wing

Take an untwisted rectangular wing (c constant) with a symmetric thin airfoil (αL=0 = 0, a₀ = 2π) and AR = b/c = 6. Write μ = πc/(2b) = π/12 and divide the monoplane equation by 1/μ:

$$\sum_{n=1,3,5,7}\hat A_n\sin n\theta_0\left(1 + \frac{n\mu}{\sin\theta_0}\right) = \alpha,\qquad \hat A_n = A_n/\mu .$$

At θ₀ = π/8, π/4, 3π/8, π/2 this is the 4 × 4 system below (Kuethe and Chow's form). Its solution is \(\hat A_1 = 0.9174\alpha\), \(\hat A_3 = 0.1104\alpha\), \(\hat A_5 = 0.0218\alpha\), \(\hat A_7 = 0.0038\alpha\), so \(A_1 = 0.2402\alpha\) and

$$C_L = \pi(6)(0.2402\alpha) = 4.5273\,\alpha,\qquad \frac{C_L}{2\pi\alpha} = 0.72,\qquad \delta = 0.0464,\qquad e = 0.956,\qquad C_{D_i} = 1.1378\,\alpha^2 .$$

The rectangular wing has only 4.6% more induced drag than an elliptic wing of the same AR and CL: not a huge penalty, but a few percent of fuel matters to an airline.

Section 2.7Key equations

Series and solution

$$y = -\tfrac b2\cos\theta,\qquad \Gamma = 2bV_\infty\sum A_n\sin n\theta$$ $$\sum A_n\sin n\theta_0\left(\frac{2b}{\pi c} + \frac{n}{\sin\theta_0}\right) = \alpha - \alpha_{L=0}$$ $$\alpha_i(\theta) = \sum nA_n\frac{\sin n\theta}{\sin\theta}$$

Results

$$C_L = \pi AR\,A_1,\qquad C_{D_i} = \pi AR\sum nA_n^2$$ $$\delta = \sum_{n\ge2}n\left(\frac{A_n}{A_1}\right)^2,\qquad e = \frac{1}{1+\delta}$$ $$C_{D_i} = \frac{C_L^2}{\pi AR}(1+\delta) = \frac{C_L^2}{\pi eAR}$$
Calculator trapsEvaluate sin nθ in radians. At the root, θ = π/2 and sin nθ = +1, −1, +1, −1 for n = 1, 3, 5, 7, so Γ(0) = 2bV∞(A₁ − A₃ + A₅ − A₇) and αi(0) = A₁ − 3A₃ + 5A₅ − 7A₇. δ starts at n = 2 (or 3 for symmetric loading), never n = 1.

Section 2.8Worked examples

Worked examples

A wing of AR 7 has A₁ = 0.0412, A₃ = 0.0049, A₅ = 0.0011 (even terms zero). Find CL, δ, e and CDi.

Solution

CL = π(7)(0.0412) = 0.9061. δ = 3(0.0049/0.0412)² + 5(0.0011/0.0412)² = 3(0.01414) + 5(0.000713) = 0.04243 + 0.00356 = 0.0460. e = 1/1.0460 = 0.956.

CDi = 0.9061²(1.0460)/(7π) = 0.03905. Check with πARΣnAₙ²: 21.99(0.001697 + 0.0000720 + 0.0000061) = 0.03905 ✓.

For the wing of Ex 2.1 with b = 12 m flying at 55 m/s at sea level, find Γ and L′ at the root.

Solution

θ = π/2: Γ(0) = 2(12)(55)(0.0412 − 0.0049 + 0.0011) = 1320(0.0374) = 49.37 m²/s. L′(0) = ρV∞Γ = 1.225(55)(49.37) = 3326 N/m.

For the same wing find the induced angle at 2y/b = 0.5 (right wing).

Solution

cos θ = −0.5, θ = 120° = 2.0944 rad. sin θ = 0.8660, sin 3θ = sin 360° = 0, sin 5θ = sin 600° = −0.8660.

αi = [0.0412(0.8660) + 3(0.0049)(0) + 5(0.0011)(−0.8660)]/0.8660 = 0.0412 − 0.0055 = 0.0357 rad = 2.05°. At the root it is 0.0412 − 0.0147 + 0.0055 = 0.0320 rad = 1.83°: not uniform, because the loading is not elliptic.

Using the AR 6 rectangular-wing results, find CL, CDi and the ratio of induced drag to that of an elliptic wing at α = 10°.

Solution

α = 0.17453 rad. CL = 4.5273(0.17453) = 0.7902. CDi = 1.1378(0.17453)² = 0.03466. Elliptic wing at the same CL: 0.7902²/(6π) = 0.03312; ratio 1.0464 = 1 + δ.

Section 2.9Check your understanding

Sketch three drag polars: elliptic AR 10, elliptic AR 20 and a "general" loading with AR 10.

Answer

All share CD0 at CL = 0. The elliptic AR 20 parabola is the narrowest; the general AR 10 wing (e < 1) is slightly wider than the elliptic AR 10 one. Aspect ratio has a much stronger effect than δ.

Why do the even coefficients vanish for a symmetric wing?

Answer

sin nθ with n even is antisymmetric about θ = π/2, so it would put more lift on one wing than the other.

A student computes δ = Σ n(Aₙ/A₁)² including n = 1. What answer do they get for an elliptic wing, and what is wrong?

Answer

δ = 1 and e = 0.5, double the true induced drag. The sum for δ starts at n = 2; the n = 1 term is the "1" in (1 + δ).

Which wing has e = 1 exactly, and why can no planar wing beat it?

Answer

The elliptically loaded one. Any other loading has some Aₙ ≠ 0 for n ≥ 2, each adding nAₙ² > 0 to CDi without adding lift.
