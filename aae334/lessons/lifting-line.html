Finite Wings and Lifting-Line Theory

An airfoil is a wing of infinite span. A real wing has tips, and around them the high pressure under the wing leaks to the low pressure above. The result is a pair of trailing vortices, a downwash over the wing, a smaller lift than the airfoil would give, and a new kind of drag. Prandtl's lifting-line theory (1918) turns this picture into one equation.

Section 1.1Wingtip vortices and downwash

A lifting wing has higher pressure on its lower surface than on its upper surface. At the tips nothing separates the two, so air curls from below to above. Combined with the oncoming freestream, this swirling motion rolls up into a trailing (wingtip) vortex behind each tip: clockwise behind the left tip and counter-clockwise behind the right tip, seen from behind.

The two vortices induce a downward velocity, the downwash \(w\), everywhere between them, including at the wing itself. Two consequences follow, and they are the whole subject of this lesson:

  1. Each wing section sees a relative wind tilted downward, so it works at a smaller angle of attack than its geometric angle and produces less lift.
  2. The local lift vector, perpendicular to the local relative wind, tilts backward. Its streamwise component is the induced drag, or drag due to lift.
Why "induced"?Induced drag exists even in inviscid flow. It is not friction and not separation: it is the rate at which the wing puts kinetic energy into the trailing vortex wake. It cannot be eliminated while the wing makes lift, only minimized.

Section 1.2Geometric, effective and induced angles

Let \(\alpha\) be the geometric angle between the chord line and the freestream \(V_\infty\). The downwash \(w\) (negative when downward) adds to the freestream and rotates the local relative wind by the induced angle of attack

$$\alpha_i = \arctan\!\left(\frac{-w}{V_\infty}\right) \approx \frac{-w}{V_\infty}\qquad(|w| \ll V_\infty).$$

The section therefore sees the effective angle of attack

$$\alpha_\text{eff} = \alpha - \alpha_i\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad \alpha = \alpha_\text{eff} + \alpha_i .$$

The local airfoil does not know it is part of a finite wing: it makes the lift its 2-D lift curve gives at \(\alpha_\text{eff}\), perpendicular to its own relative wind. Resolving that force in the freestream axes gives a lift \(L' \cos\alpha_i \approx L'\) and a drag \(D_i' = L'\sin\alpha_i \approx L'\alpha_i\).

Section 1.3The vortex system: horseshoe vortices

Lift on a 2-D airfoil comes from its circulation Γ, through the Kutta–Joukowski theorem \(L' = \rho_\infty V_\infty\Gamma\). To model a wing, Prandtl replaced it by a bound vortex along the quarter-chord line. Helmholtz's vortex theorems say the strength of a vortex filament is constant along it and that it cannot end in the fluid, so the bound vortex must turn at the tips and trail downstream to infinity: a horseshoe vortex.

A single horseshoe has constant Γ across the span, so the lift per unit span would be uniform, and the downwash it induces on the wing is infinite at the tips, where the trailing legs are. That is unphysical. The fix is to superimpose many horseshoes of different spans: wherever the bound circulation changes by \(d\Gamma\) along the span, a trailing filament of strength \(d\Gamma\) is shed. In the limit, the wing is a lifting line with continuous circulation Γ(y), and it sheds a trailing vortex sheet whose strength per unit span is \(-d\Gamma/dy\).

Section 1.4The downwash integral

The Biot–Savart law gives the velocity induced by a straight vortex filament. An infinite line vortex of strength Γ induces \(\Gamma/(2\pi h)\) at distance \(h\). A semi-infinite filament that starts on the lifting line at \(y\) and runs downstream induces half that at a point \(y_0\) on the line, because it only extends one way:

$$dw(y_0) = -\frac{(d\Gamma/dy)\,dy}{4\pi(y_0 - y)} .$$

(The sign makes \(w\) negative, i.e., downward, inboard of a tip where Γ is falling.) Adding the contributions of the whole trailing sheet,

$$w(y_0) = -\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{-b/2}^{b/2}\frac{(d\Gamma/dy)}{y_0 - y}\,dy,\qquad \alpha_i(y_0) = \frac{1}{4\pi V_\infty}\int_{-b/2}^{b/2}\frac{(d\Gamma/dy)}{y_0 - y}\,dy .$$

The integral is a Cauchy principal value: the integrand is singular at \(y = y_0\), and the two sides cancel symmetrically. The bound vortex itself induces nothing on its own line, so only the trailing sheet produces downwash.

Section 1.5Prandtl's lifting-line equation

Now connect the circulation to the section aerodynamics. Each section follows its 2-D lift curve at the effective angle of attack, with slope \(a_0\) (\(= 2\pi\) for thin-airfoil theory):

$$c_\ell(y_0) = a_0\left[\alpha_\text{eff}(y_0) - \alpha_{L=0}(y_0)\right].$$

Kutta–Joukowski strip by strip gives the same section lift in terms of Γ: \(L' = \rho_\infty V_\infty \Gamma = \tfrac12\rho_\infty V_\infty^2\,c\,c_\ell\), so

$$c_\ell(y_0) = \frac{2\Gamma(y_0)}{V_\infty\,c(y_0)} .$$

Equating the two, solving for \(\alpha_\text{eff}\) and using \(\alpha = \alpha_\text{eff} + \alpha_i\) gives Prandtl's lifting-line equation (with \(a_0 = 2\pi\)):

$$\boxed{\;\alpha(y_0) = \underbrace{\frac{\Gamma(y_0)}{\pi V_\infty c(y_0)} + \alpha_{L=0}(y_0)}_{\alpha_\text{eff}} \;+\; \underbrace{\frac{1}{4\pi V_\infty}\int_{-b/2}^{b/2}\frac{(d\Gamma/dy)}{y_0 - y}\,dy}_{\alpha_i}\;}$$

For a general \(a_0\), the first term is \(2\Gamma/(a_0V_\infty c)\). Given the wing geometry, \(\alpha(y)\), \(c(y)\) and \(\alpha_{L=0}(y)\), the only unknown is Γ(y). It is an integro-differential equation: the induced angle depends on the lift, and the lift depends on the induced angle. Lesson 2 solves it for any wing; here we solve it for the most important case.

Section 1.6Lift and induced drag from Γ(y)

Once Γ(y) is known, everything follows by integrating across the span:

$$L'(y) = \rho_\infty V_\infty\Gamma(y),\qquad L = \rho_\infty V_\infty\int_{-b/2}^{b/2}\Gamma\,dy,\qquad C_L = \frac{2}{V_\infty S}\int_{-b/2}^{b/2}\Gamma(y)\,dy,$$ $$D_i = \int_{-b/2}^{b/2} L'(y)\,\alpha_i(y)\,dy,\qquad C_{D_i} = \frac{2}{V_\infty S}\int_{-b/2}^{b/2}\Gamma(y)\,\alpha_i(y)\,dy .$$

Section 1.7Elliptic loading

Take the circulation to be elliptic, with root value Γ₀:

$$\Gamma(y) = \Gamma_0\sqrt{1 - \left(\frac{2y}{b}\right)^2}.$$

Downwash. Substitute \(y = -\tfrac b2\cos\theta\) (so \(\sqrt{1-(2y/b)^2} = \sin\theta\) and \(\Gamma = \Gamma_0\sin\theta\)). Then \((d\Gamma/dy)\,dy = \Gamma_0\cos\theta\,d\theta\) and \(y_0 - y = \tfrac b2(\cos\theta - \cos\theta_0)\), so

$$w(\theta_0) = -\frac{\Gamma_0}{2\pi b}\int_0^\pi\frac{\cos\theta}{\cos\theta - \cos\theta_0}\,d\theta = -\frac{\Gamma_0}{2\pi b}\cdot\pi = -\frac{\Gamma_0}{2b}.$$

The integral is Glauert's, \(\int_0^\pi \frac{\cos n\theta\,d\theta}{\cos\theta - \cos\theta_0} = \pi\frac{\sin n\theta_0}{\sin\theta_0}\), with \(n = 1\). The downwash is the same at every station, and so is the induced angle, \(\alpha_i = \Gamma_0/(2bV_\infty)\).

Lift. \(\int_{-b/2}^{b/2}\sqrt{1-(2y/b)^2}\,dy = \pi b/4\), so \(L = \rho_\infty V_\infty\Gamma_0\pi b/4\) and

$$C_L = \frac{\pi b\,\Gamma_0}{2V_\infty S}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad \Gamma_0 = \frac{2V_\infty S C_L}{\pi b},\qquad \alpha_i = \frac{\Gamma_0}{2bV_\infty} = \frac{C_L}{\pi AR},\qquad AR = \frac{b^2}{S}.$$

Induced drag. Because αi is constant it comes out of the integral: \(C_{D_i} = \alpha_i\cdot\frac{2}{V_\infty S}\int\Gamma\,dy = \alpha_i C_L\), so

$$\boxed{\;C_{D_i} = \frac{C_L^2}{\pi AR}\;}$$

Induced drag grows with the square of the lift and falls inversely with aspect ratio. In the 2-D limit \(AR \to\infty\) it vanishes. Lesson 2 shows that, for a given span and lift, the elliptic distribution has the least induced drag of any planar wing.

Constant section lift. If the wing is untwisted (α constant) and has the same airfoil everywhere (αL=0 constant), then αeff = α − αi is constant along the span, and so is \(c_\ell\). Since \(C_L = \frac1S\int c_\ell c\,dy\), the wing's \(C_L\) equals the section \(c_\ell\). With \(L' \propto c\,c_\ell\) elliptic and \(c_\ell\) constant, the chord must be elliptic: an untwisted wing with elliptic loading has an elliptic planform (the Spitfire's wing is the famous example).

Section 1.8Lift slope and aspect ratio

For that wing, \(C_L = c_\ell = a_0(\alpha - \alpha_i - \alpha_{L=0}) = a_0\left(\alpha - \alpha_{L=0} - \frac{C_L}{\pi AR}\right)\). Solving for \(C_L\):

$$C_L = \frac{a_0}{1 + a_0/(\pi AR)}\,(\alpha - \alpha_{L=0}),\qquad a = \frac{dC_L}{d\alpha} = \frac{a_0}{1 + a_0/(\pi AR)} \xrightarrow{a_0 = 2\pi} \frac{2\pi}{1 + 2/AR}.$$

The ratio \(a/a_0\) is 0.67 at AR 4, 0.80 at AR 8 and 0.875 at AR 14. Two wings with the same airfoil but different aspect ratios therefore have lift curves that cross the axis at the same αL=0 but with different slopes, and drag polars \(C_D = c_d + C_L^2/(\pi AR)\) that share the same minimum but open more widely at low AR. High aspect ratio helps on both counts, which is why gliders, the U-2/ER-2, Global Hawk and solar aircraft have long, slender wings; structural weight and airport gates limit it on transports.

Units trapIn \(a_0/(\pi AR)\), \(a_0\) must be per radian. If a problem gives \(a_0\) per degree, multiply by 180/π first, and convert the answer back if you want per degree.

Section 1.9Key equations and assumptions

Lifting line

$$\alpha = \alpha_\text{eff} + \alpha_i,\qquad \alpha_i \approx -\frac{w}{V_\infty}$$ $$c_\ell = \frac{2\Gamma}{V_\infty c} = a_0(\alpha_\text{eff} - \alpha_{L=0})$$ $$w(y_0) = -\frac1{4\pi}\int_{-b/2}^{b/2}\frac{d\Gamma/dy}{y_0-y}dy$$ $$C_L = \frac{2}{V_\infty S}\int\Gamma\,dy,\quad C_{D_i} = \frac{2}{V_\infty S}\int\Gamma\alpha_i\,dy$$

Elliptic loading

$$\Gamma = \Gamma_0\sqrt{1-(2y/b)^2},\qquad w = -\frac{\Gamma_0}{2b}$$ $$\Gamma_0 = \frac{2V_\infty SC_L}{\pi b},\qquad \alpha_i = \frac{C_L}{\pi AR}$$ $$C_{D_i} = \frac{C_L^2}{\pi AR},\qquad a = \frac{a_0}{1 + a_0/(\pi AR)}$$
Assumptions of lifting-line theorySteady, inviscid, incompressible flow; attached flow on every section (no stall); each section acts like a 2-D airfoil at its local αeff with a linear lift curve; small angles (αi ≪ 1, w ≪ V∞); a flat trailing wake leaving in the freestream direction; a straight, unswept (or nearly unswept) lifting line on a wing of moderate-to-high aspect ratio. It is not valid for highly swept or low-AR (delta) wings, which need lifting-surface (vortex-lattice) methods.

Section 1.10Worked examples

Worked examples

A 1500 kg airplane cruises at 70 m/s at sea level. Its untwisted elliptic wing has a span of 11 m and S = 16 m². Find CL, Γ₀, w, αi, CDi and Di.

Solution

AR = 11²/16 = 7.5625; q∞ = ½(1.225)(70²) = 3001 Pa; CL = 1500(9.807)/(3001 × 16) = 0.3063.

Γ₀ = 2V∞SCL/(πb) = 2(70)(16)(0.3063)/(π·11) = 19.86 m²/s; w = −Γ₀/(2b) = −0.903 m/s.

αi = CL/(πAR) = 0.01289 rad = 0.739° (check: −w/V∞ = 0.903/70 = 0.0129 ✓).

CDi = CL²/(πAR) = 0.003949; Di = q∞SCDi = 189.6 N, which also equals Wαi = 14710(0.01289) ✓.

An airfoil has a₀ = 0.105 per degree and αL=0 = −1.5°. Find the lift slope (per degree) of an untwisted elliptic wing with AR = 6 made from it, and CL at α = 6°.

Solution

a₀ = 0.105 × 57.30 = 6.016 rad⁻¹. a = 6.016/(1 + 6.016/(6π)) = 6.016/1.3192 = 4.560 rad⁻¹ = 0.0796 per degree.

CL = 0.0796(6 − (−1.5)) = 0.597. The common error is a = 0.105/(1 + 0.105/(6π)) = 0.1044 per degree, which barely differs from the airfoil.

Two untwisted elliptic wings with the same thin symmetric airfoil have AR = 5 and AR = 10. At CL = 0.6 compare α and CDi.

Solution

Slopes: 2π/(1 + 2/5) = 4.488 and 2π/(1 + 2/10) = 5.236 rad⁻¹. Angles: 0.6/4.488 = 0.1337 rad = 7.66° and 0.6/5.236 = 6.57°.

CDi = 0.36/(5π) = 0.02292 and 0.36/(10π) = 0.01146: doubling AR halves the induced drag at the same lift. The effective angle (0.6/2π = 5.47°) is the same for both; only αi (2.19° vs 1.09°) differs.

An airplane with an elliptic wing flies at constant speed and altitude. During the flight it burns 15% of its weight in fuel. By how much do CL, CDi and Di change?

Solution

CL = W/(q∞S) falls by 15% (factor 0.85). CDi ∝ CL² falls by a factor 0.7225 (−27.75%). Di = q∞SCDi falls by the same factor; equivalently Di = W²/(q∞πb²), so the induced drag depends on the span loading W/b, not on AR by itself.

Section 1.11Check your understanding

Sketch a wing and its tip vortices, and state in one sentence how they change the lift and drag.

Answer

The tip vortices induce downwash over the wing, which lowers the effective angle of attack (less lift than the airfoil at the same α) and tilts the lift vector back (induced drag).

Why can a single horseshoe vortex not represent a real wing?

Answer

It has constant Γ (uniform loading) and its trailing legs sit at the tips, where they induce infinite downwash. Superposing many horseshoes gives a smooth Γ(y) that falls to zero at the tips.

Name the three terms of Prandtl's equation.

Answer

Geometric angle α (left side) = effective angle Γ/(πV∞c) + αL=0, plus the induced angle given by the downwash integral.

For an untwisted wing with an elliptic planform, how does cℓ vary along the span?

Answer

It is constant and equal to CL, because α, αL=0 and αi are all constant.

Draw the lift curves and drag polars of two rectangular untwisted wings with the same airfoil and AR = 4 and AR = 12.

Answer

Lift curves: both pass through the same αL=0; the AR 12 line is steeper (closer to the airfoil's), and the AR 4 wing reaches stall at a higher α. Polars: same CD at CL = 0, but the AR 4 parabola is wider (more drag at any nonzero CL).
