Pitot Probes

A Pitot tube is a tube pointed into the flow, closed at the back, so the gas in its mouth is brought to rest. It is how airliners measure airspeed and how wind tunnels measure Mach number. What it reads, and which formula turns the reading into a speed, depends on whether the flow is incompressible, subsonic compressible or supersonic.

Section 9.1What a Pitot probe measures

The gas that enters the probe mouth is decelerated to rest, smoothly and without heat transfer, so the pressure transducer at the back reads a stagnation pressure. A separate measurement of the static pressure (from a static port on the side of the probe, a Pitot-static tube, or a wall tap) gives the second number needed. A thermocouple stagnation-temperature probe gives T₀, from which the static temperature, and hence the speed of sound and velocity, follow. (The device is named for Henri Pitot, 1732; Henry Darcy added the static port in the 1850s.)

Section 9.2Incompressible flow: Bernoulli

At low speed (M < 0.3) Bernoulli's equation along the stagnation streamline gives

$$p_0 = p_\infty + \tfrac12\rho_\infty V_\infty^2\quad\Longrightarrow\quad V_\infty = \sqrt{\frac{2(p_0 - p_\infty)}{\rho_\infty}} .$$

The density is needed: from \(\rho = p/(RT)\) with a separate temperature measurement, or from a known atmosphere. Airspeed indicators are calibrated this way with sea-level density, which is why they read "indicated" airspeed.

Section 9.3Subsonic compressible flow

Above M ≈ 0.3, Bernoulli's equation is no longer accurate (Lesson 7). The deceleration into the probe is still isentropic, so

$$\frac{p_0}{p_\infty} = \left(1 + \frac{\gamma - 1}{2}M_\infty^2\right)^{\gamma/(\gamma-1)}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad M_\infty = \sqrt{\frac{2}{\gamma - 1}\left[\left(\frac{p_0}{p_\infty}\right)^{(\gamma-1)/\gamma} - 1\right]} .$$

The pressure ratio gives the Mach number, not the velocity. For the velocity we need the temperature: a thermocouple probe reads T₀, then

$$T_\infty = \frac{T_0}{1 + \frac{\gamma-1}{2}M_\infty^2},\qquad V_\infty = M_\infty\sqrt{\gamma RT_\infty} .$$

This assumes there are no shocks, i.e. the freestream is subsonic.

Section 9.4Supersonic flow: the Rayleigh–Pitot formula

In supersonic flow a detached bow shock forms ahead of the probe. On the stagnation streamline it is a normal shock. The gas is therefore processed in two steps: from 1 to 2 by the normal-shock jump, then from 2 to 3 (the probe mouth) isentropically to rest. The probe reads p₃ = p₀₂, not p₀₁. Combining the shock relation for p₂/p₁ and M₂ with the isentropic relation for p₀₂/p₂ gives the Rayleigh–Pitot formula:

$$\frac{p_{02}}{p_1} = \frac{p_{02}}{p_2}\cdot\frac{p_2}{p_1} = \left[\frac{(\gamma + 1)^2M_1^2}{4\gamma M_1^2 - 2(\gamma - 1)}\right]^{\gamma/(\gamma-1)}\frac{1 - \gamma + 2\gamma M_1^2}{\gamma + 1}.$$

It is tabulated in the normal-shock table (Appendix B) as the column p₀₂/p₁. Given the measured ratio, find M₁ from the table (closest entry) or by iteration. The thermocouple still gives the freestream T₀, because the shock is adiabatic (T₀₂ = T₀₁), so T₁ and V₁ follow exactly as in the subsonic case. Warning: the formula is valid only because the shock on the stagnation streamline is normal.

Section 9.5Which formula? The 1.893 test

At M = 1 both formulas give the same ratio, p₀/p = (1.2)3.5 = 1.893. Since both ratios increase with M:

  • \(p_\text{Pitot}/p_\infty < 1.893\): subsonic, use the isentropic relation.
  • \(p_\text{Pitot}/p_\infty > 1.893\): supersonic, use Rayleigh–Pitot.

Using the isentropic relation in supersonic flow underestimates the Mach number (a ratio of 5.64 would give M = 1.79 instead of 2.00), because it ignores the stagnation-pressure loss through the shock.

Section 9.6Measuring the static pressure

Behind the bow shock the static pressure is p₂, not p₁, so a static port on the probe's side might seem to read the wrong value. In practice the static holes are placed well downstream of the nose (about ten diameters), where the bow shock has weakened through its interaction with the expansion waves around the probe shoulder to nearly a Mach wave, and the pressure there is close to p₁. In wind tunnels the freestream static pressure is usually measured with a wall tap instead. In problems, "the static pressure" means p₁ unless stated otherwise.

Section 9.7Key equations and procedure

Procedure

1. Form pPitot/p∞ and compare with 1.893.

2. Subsonic: M from p₀/p = (1 + 0.2M²)3.5. Supersonic: M₁ from the p₀₂/p₁ column (Rayleigh–Pitot).

3. T∞ = T₀/(1 + 0.2M²) using the thermocouple T₀ (unchanged by the shock).

4. V∞ = M√(γRT∞). If asked, p₀∞ = p∞(1 + 0.2M²)3.5 (the freestream value, larger than the Pitot reading in supersonic flow).

Equations

$$V = \sqrt{2(p_0 - p)/\rho}\ \ (M < 0.3)$$ $$\frac{p_0}{p} = \left(1 + \tfrac{\gamma-1}2M^2\right)^{\frac{\gamma}{\gamma-1}}\ \ (M < 1)$$ $$\frac{p_{02}}{p_1} = \left[\frac{(\gamma+1)^2M_1^2}{4\gamma M_1^2 - 2(\gamma-1)}\right]^{\frac{\gamma}{\gamma-1}}\frac{1 - \gamma + 2\gamma M_1^2}{\gamma+1}\ \ (M > 1)$$

Section 9.8Worked examples

Worked examples

A Pitot probe reads 72.6 kPa, the static pressure is 50.0 kPa and a stagnation thermocouple reads 300 K. Find M∞, T∞ and V∞. What would Bernoulli give?

Solution

Ratio 1.452 < 1.893: subsonic. Isentropic table: p₀/p = 1.452 → M = 0.75 (closest entry; exact 0.7498). T∞ = 300/(1 + 0.2·0.5625) = 269.7 K, a = 329.2 m/s, V = 246.8 m/s.

Bernoulli: ρ = 50000/(287·269.7) = 0.6460 kg/m³, V = √(2·22600/0.6460) = 264.5 m/s, 7% too high.

In a wind tunnel the Pitot probe reads 282 kPa, a wall tap reads 50.0 kPa, and T₀ = 300 K. Find M∞, T∞ and V∞.

Solution

Ratio 5.64 > 1.893: supersonic. Normal-shock table, p₀₂/p₁ = 5.640 → M₁ = 2.00. T∞ = 300/1.8 = 166.7 K, a = 258.8 m/s, V = 517.5 m/s. (The isentropic formula would have given M = 1.79, 10% low.)

For Ex 9.2, find the freestream stagnation pressure and the stagnation-pressure loss through the probe's bow shock.

Solution

p₀₁ = 50.0(7.824) = 391.2 kPa (isentropic table at M = 2). The probe reads p₀₂ = 282 kPa, so p₀₂/p₀₁ = 0.721 (the table value at M = 2 is 0.7209 ✓): 28% of the stagnation pressure is lost.

A Pitot-static tube in a low-speed tunnel reads p₀ − p = 1.20 kPa; the air is at 101 kPa and 295 K. Find V and check that Bernoulli is justified.

Solution

ρ = 101000/(287·295) = 1.193 kg/m³; V = √(2·1200/1.193) = 44.85 m/s; a = 344.3 m/s, M = 0.13 < 0.3 ✓ (the compressible answer differs by about 0.2%).

Section 9.9Check your understanding

Why does a Pitot probe in supersonic flow read less than the freestream stagnation pressure?

Answer

The gas passes through the normal part of the bow shock first, losing stagnation pressure; the probe reads p₀₂ < p₀₁.

The probe gives M. What else do you need for V, and why is a thermocouple reading in supersonic flow still useful?

Answer

The static temperature (for a). A thermocouple gives T₀, which is unchanged across the adiabatic shock, so T∞ = T₀/(1 + 0.2M²).

A probe reads pPitot/p = 1.80. Which formula, and roughly what M?

Answer

Subsonic (1.80 < 1.893): isentropic, M ≈ 0.96.
