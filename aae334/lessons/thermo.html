Thermodynamics Review

Above about Mach 0.3 the density of air changes noticeably, and mass and momentum conservation become coupled to the energy equation. Before we can analyze compressible flow we need the thermodynamics of a perfect gas: its equation of state, its energies, the first and second laws, and the isentropic relations that come out of them.

Section 6.1Why thermodynamics now

In incompressible flow the density is a constant and pressure and velocity are linked by Bernoulli's equation; temperature is a passenger. In a high-speed flow, slowing the gas down compresses and heats it, so the density changes and the energy equation must be solved together with mass and momentum. The rule of thumb is that compressibility matters for M > 0.3, where the stagnation density is about 5% above the freestream value (Lesson 7). Compressible flow also predicts new phenomena: a finite speed of sound and a limited zone of influence, linear sound waves, and nonlinear shock waves.

We take a macroscopic view: the gas is a continuum in local thermodynamic equilibrium, so at every point two properties fix the state (for example \(p = p(\rho, T)\)), and the bulk motion does not distort the random molecular motion.

Section 6.2The perfect gas

A perfect (ideal) gas has negligible intermolecular forces except during collisions. Its equation of state can be written per mole, per molecule or per unit mass:

$$pV = n\mathcal R T,\qquad p = nk_BT\ \ (n\text{ per volume}),\qquad p = \rho RT,\qquad pv = RT,$$

with \(\mathcal R = 8314\ \mathrm{J/(kmol\,K)}\), \(k_B = 1.381\times10^{-23}\ \mathrm{J/K}\), specific volume \(v = 1/\rho\) (not velocity!) and the specific gas constant \(R = \mathcal R/\mathcal M = k_B/m\). For air, \(\mathcal M = 28.97\) kg/kmol and

$$R_\text{air} = 287\ \mathrm{J/(kg\,K)} = 1716\ \mathrm{ft\,lbf/(slug\,{}^\circ R)}.$$

Section 6.3Internal energy, enthalpy and specific heats

The specific internal energy \(e\) (also written u) is the energy of random molecular motion per unit mass. The specific enthalpy adds the flow work:

$$h = e + pv = e + \frac p\rho .$$

The specific heats are the temperature derivatives at constant volume and constant pressure:

$$c_v = \left(\frac{\partial e}{\partial T}\right)_v,\qquad c_p = \left(\frac{\partial h}{\partial T}\right)_p .$$

In general \(e = e(\rho, T)\). A thermally perfect gas has \(e = e(T)\) and \(h = h(T)\) only; then \(h = e + RT\) gives \(c_p - c_v = R\). A calorically perfect gas also has constant specific heats, so

$$e = c_vT,\qquad h = c_pT,\qquad \gamma = \frac{c_p}{c_v},\qquad c_v = \frac{R}{\gamma - 1},\qquad c_p = \frac{\gamma R}{\gamma - 1}.$$

For air: cp = 1004.5 J/(kg K) ≈ 6006 ft·lbf/(slug·°R), cv = 717.5 J/(kg K). Don't confuse cp (specific heat) with Cp (pressure coefficient).

Section 6.4γ and molecular degrees of freedom

Each fully excited quadratic energy mode holds \(\tfrac12k_BT\) per molecule (equipartition), so with n active degrees of freedom \(c_v = \tfrac n2R\) and

$$\gamma = \frac{c_p}{c_v} = \frac{n + 2}{n}.$$
  • Monatomic (He, Ar): 3 translational modes, γ = 5/3 = 1.667.
  • Diatomic (N₂, O₂, air) near room temperature: 3 translation + 2 rotation, γ = 7/5 = 1.4. Vibration (2 more: kinetic and potential) starts to contribute above about 600 K; fully excited, γ → 9/7 = 1.29 (around 2000 K and up).
  • Polyatomic (CO₂ γ = 1.289, steam 1.327, octane 1.044): more rotational and vibrational modes, γ closer to 1.
  • An incompressible substance has γ = 1.

So for gases 1 < γ ≤ 5/3, and γ = 1.4 for air at "typical" temperatures. At very high temperature electronic excitation, dissociation and ionization appear (the hypersonics lessons on this site cover them).

Section 6.5The first law and kinds of process

For a fixed mass (a system moving with the flow), energy is conserved:

$$\delta q + \delta w = de \qquad (\text{heat added} + \text{work done on the system} = \text{change in internal energy}).$$

\(de\) depends only on the end states; \(\delta q\) and \(\delta w\) depend on the process. The kinds of process you must be able to distinguish, with an example of each:

  • Adiabatic: no heat transfer, \(\delta q = 0\). Example: flow through a well-insulated duct, or through a shock wave.
  • Reversible: no dissipative effects (viscosity, heat conduction across finite ΔT, diffusion), so the system can be returned to its initial state with no change to the surroundings. Then \(\delta w = -p\,dv\) and \(\delta q = de + p\,dv\). Example: slow, frictionless compression in a cylinder (with heat exchange at nearly equal temperatures).
  • Isentropic: \(ds = 0\), which requires both adiabatic and reversible. Example: the flow outside the boundary layer in a nozzle, or a sound wave.

Section 6.6The second law and entropy

Entropy measures molecular disorder. It is defined through reversible heat transfer, and real processes add an irreversible part:

$$ds = \frac{\delta q_\text{rev}}{T},\qquad ds = \frac{\delta q}{T} + ds_\text{irrev},\qquad ds_\text{irrev} \ge 0\ \Rightarrow\ ds \ge \frac{\delta q}{T}.$$

For an adiabatic process \(ds \ge 0\), with equality only if it is also reversible. Entropy is raised by heat addition and by irreversibility; it can be lowered locally only by cooling (\(\delta q < 0\)).

Section 6.7Entropy change of a perfect gas

Derivation

For a reversible path between the same two states, \(\delta q = T\,ds\) and \(\delta w = -p\,dv\), so the first law gives the Gibbs equation, which relates properties and therefore holds for any process:

$$T\,ds = de + p\,dv .$$

Since \(dh = de + p\,dv + v\,dp\), the second form is \(T\,ds = dh - v\,dp\). Divide by T and use \(de = c_vdT\), \(dh = c_pdT\), \(p/T = R/v\), \(v/T = R/p\):

$$ds = c_v\frac{dT}{T} + R\frac{dv}{v} = c_p\frac{dT}{T} - R\frac{dp}{p}.$$

Integrate with constant specific heats:

$$\boxed{\;s_2 - s_1 = c_p\ln\frac{T_2}{T_1} - R\ln\frac{p_2}{p_1} = c_v\ln\frac{T_2}{T_1} + R\ln\frac{v_2}{v_1}\;}$$

Section 6.8Isentropic relations

At high Reynolds numbers, viscous dissipation and heat conduction are confined to thin boundary layers (and shocks). Elsewhere the flow is adiabatic and nearly reversible, i.e. isentropic. Setting \(s_2 = s_1\) in the first form and using \(c_p/R = \gamma/(\gamma - 1)\):

$$\frac{p_2}{p_1} = \left(\frac{T_2}{T_1}\right)^{\gamma/(\gamma-1)} .$$

The second form with \(c_v/R = 1/(\gamma - 1)\) gives \(T_2/T_1 = (v_2/v_1)^{-(\gamma-1)} = (\rho_2/\rho_1)^{\gamma-1}\). Together:

$$\boxed{\;\frac{p_2}{p_1} = \left(\frac{\rho_2}{\rho_1}\right)^\gamma = \left(\frac{T_2}{T_1}\right)^{\gamma/(\gamma-1)}\;}\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad pv^\gamma = \text{const}.$$

γ is the "adiabatic exponent". For air, \(\gamma/(\gamma - 1) = 3.5\) and \(1/(\gamma - 1) = 2.5\).

Section 6.9Key equations

Perfect gas

$$p = \rho RT,\quad R = \mathcal R/\mathcal M,\quad h = e + p/\rho$$ $$c_p - c_v = R,\quad c_v = \frac{R}{\gamma-1},\quad c_p = \frac{\gamma R}{\gamma - 1}$$ $$\gamma = \frac{n+2}{n}:\ \ 5/3,\ 7/5,\ 9/7$$

Laws and processes

$$\delta q + \delta w = de,\qquad ds \ge \delta q/T$$ $$s_2 - s_1 = c_p\ln\frac{T_2}{T_1} - R\ln\frac{p_2}{p_1}$$ $$\frac{p_2}{p_1} = \left(\frac{\rho_2}{\rho_1}\right)^\gamma = \left(\frac{T_2}{T_1}\right)^{\frac{\gamma}{\gamma-1}}$$

Section 6.10Worked examples

Worked examples

Find R, cv, cp, ρ, e and h for carbon dioxide (ℳ = 44.01, γ = 1.289) at 400 K and 200 kPa.

Solution

R = 8314/44.01 = 188.9 J/(kg K); cv = 188.9/0.289 = 653.6; cp = 1.289(653.6) = 842.5 J/(kg K). ρ = 200000/(188.9·400) = 2.647 kg/m³. e = cvT = 261.4 kJ/kg; h = cpT = 337.0 kJ/kg (check: h − e = RT = 75.6 kJ/kg ✓).

Air at 101.3 kPa and 288 K is compressed isentropically to 600 kPa. Find T₂ and ρ₂/ρ₁.

Solution

T₂ = 288(600/101.3)0.2857 = 288(5.923)0.2857 = 288(1.6623) = 478.7 K. ρ₂/ρ₁ = (p₂/p₁)1/γ = 5.9230.7143 = 3.563 (check: p₂/p₁ = (ρ₂/ρ₁)(T₂/T₁) = 3.563 × 1.662 = 5.923 ✓).

Air goes from 300 K, 100 kPa to 500 K, 500 kPa. Find Δs. Could the process have been adiabatic?

Solution

Δs = 1004.5 ln(5/3) − 287 ln 5 = 513.1 − 461.9 = 51.2 J/(kg K). Δs > 0, so an adiabatic irreversible process (e.g., a real compressor) could do it. The isentropic end temperature would be 300(5)0.2857 = 475 K; the extra 25 K is the dissipation.

Classify: (a) air in an insulated cylinder compressed rapidly with friction; (b) flow through a normal shock; (c) the inviscid core of a wind-tunnel nozzle; (d) a gas cooled at constant volume.

Solution

(a) adiabatic, irreversible (Δs > 0); (b) adiabatic, irreversible (Δs > 0, T₀ constant); (c) isentropic; (d) reversible heat removal possible, Δs < 0 (entropy can fall only by cooling).

Section 6.11Check your understanding

Explain the difference between internal energy and enthalpy.

Answer

e is the energy of the molecules' random motion; h = e + p/ρ adds the flow work needed to push the fluid into a control volume. In a flow, h (and h₀) is the natural energy variable.

State the range of γ for gases and its value for air at typical temperatures.

Answer

1 < γ ≤ 5/3; air 1.4 (falls toward 1.29 when vibration is excited, above ~600 K).

True or false: an adiabatic process is always isentropic.

Answer

False. Adiabatic means no heat transfer; isentropic also requires reversibility. A shock is adiabatic but not isentropic.

Derive p₂/p₁ = (T₂/T₁)γ/(γ−1).

Answer

Set Δs = cpln(T₂/T₁) − R ln(p₂/p₁) = 0 and use cp/R = γ/(γ − 1).
