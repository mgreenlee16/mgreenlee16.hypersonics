Quasi-1D Flow and Nozzles

What happens to a compressible flow when the duct area changes? In subsonic flow a contraction speeds the gas up, as you would expect; in supersonic flow it slows it down. This one fact explains why rockets and supersonic wind tunnels use converging–diverging nozzles, why there is a throat where M = 1, and why a nozzle "chokes".

Section 10.1Quasi-one-dimensional flow

Assume the flow is uniform over each cross-section A(x) but varies along the duct: quasi-1D flow. It is steady, inviscid, adiabatic, with no body forces, so outside shocks it is isentropic. Conservation of mass for a control volume between two sections gives \(\rho_1u_1A_1 = \rho_2u_2A_2\), i.e. \(\rho uA = \dot m = \text{const}\). For incompressible flow, ρ is constant and u ∝ 1/A. For compressible flow, the density also changes, and that is where the surprise comes from.

Section 10.2The area–velocity relation

Derivation

Take logarithms of ρuA = const and differentiate: \(\dfrac{d\rho}{\rho} + \dfrac{du}{u} + \dfrac{dA}{A} = 0\).

Momentum for a short element (inviscid, steady; after subtracting u × mass conservation) gives Euler's equation \(dp = -\rho u\,du\).

For isentropic flow \(dp = a^2d\rho\), so \(\dfrac{d\rho}{\rho} = -\dfrac{u\,du}{a^2} = -M^2\dfrac{du}{u}\). Substitute into the mass equation:

$$\boxed{\;\frac{dA}{A} = \left(M^2 - 1\right)\frac{du}{u}\;}$$
dA < 0 (converging)dA > 0 (diverging)
M < 1du > 0: accelerates (nozzle)du < 0: decelerates (diffuser)
M > 1du < 0: decelerates (diffuser)du > 0: accelerates (nozzle)

In supersonic flow the density falls faster than the velocity rises, so the area must grow to pass the same mass flow. At M = 1 the relation requires dA = 0: sonic flow can occur only at a minimum area, the throat. To accelerate a gas from rest to supersonic speed you need a converging–diverging (de Laval) nozzle with M = 1 at the throat. (The reverse, an ideal supersonic diffuser that decelerates isentropically through a sonic throat, is unstable in practice; real diffusers use shocks.)

Section 10.3The area–Mach number relation

Write mass conservation between a section and a (possibly imaginary) sonic section of area A*: \(\rho uA = \rho^*a^*A^*\). Using the isentropic ratios of Lesson 7,

$$\left(\frac{A}{A^*}\right)^2 = \frac{1}{M^2}\left[\frac{2}{\gamma + 1}\left(1 + \frac{\gamma - 1}{2}M^2\right)\right]^{(\gamma+1)/(\gamma-1)} .$$

A/A* ≥ 1, with its minimum 1 at M = 1. For each value above 1 there are two solutions, one subsonic and one supersonic; which one occurs depends on the pressures at the ends of the nozzle. A* does not have to exist physically: it is a reference area, equal to the throat area only when the throat is sonic. Typical values: A/A* = 1.688 at M = 2 and 10.72 at M = 4, so a high-Mach wind tunnel needs a very small throat.

Section 10.4Back pressure and nozzle flow regimes

A converging–diverging nozzle fed from a reservoir (p₀, T₀) exhausts into a region at the back pressure pb. If the exit flow is subsonic, the exit pressure must equal pb; if it is supersonic, it need not. Lowering pb from p₀ (Interactive 2):

  1. Subsonic isentropic: the flow accelerates to a maximum M at the throat (below 1) and decelerates again. The mass flow grows as pb falls.
  2. Choked, subsonic exit: the throat just reaches M = 1, but the diverging part is still a subsonic diffuser (the subsonic solution of A/A*).
  3. Normal shock in the diverging section: supersonic after the throat, then a shock, then subsonic deceleration to pb. Lower pb moves the shock downstream.
  4. Normal shock at the exit.
  5. Overexpanded (pe < pb): the flow inside is fully supersonic and isentropic; oblique shocks (or a Mach disk) outside raise the pressure to pb.
  6. Perfectly expanded (pe = pb): the design condition.
  7. Underexpanded (pe > pb): expansion fans outside the exit (always the case in space).

From regime 2 onward the throat is sonic and the flow upstream of the throat no longer changes: the nozzle is choked.

Section 10.5Choking and mass flow

For a converging nozzle, lowering pb/p₀ increases the exit Mach number and the mass flow until the exit is sonic, at pb/p₀ = p*/p₀ = 0.528 (nozzle pressure ratio p₀/pb = 1.893). Further reductions in pb change nothing inside the nozzle: the signal cannot travel upstream through the sonic exit. The mass flow is then fixed. Evaluating ρuA at the sonic throat,

$$\dot m_\text{choked} = \rho^*a^*A^* = \frac{p_0A^*}{\sqrt{T_0}}\sqrt{\frac{\gamma}{R}}\left(\frac{2}{\gamma + 1}\right)^{(\gamma+1)/(2(\gamma-1))} = 0.0404\,\frac{p_0A^*}{\sqrt{T_0}}\ \ (\text{SI, air}).$$

For an unchoked nozzle, the exit pressure equals the back pressure: find Me from \(p_0/p_e = (1 + \tfrac{\gamma-1}{2}M_e^2)^{\gamma/(\gamma-1)}\), then

$$\dot m = \frac{p_0A_e}{\sqrt{RT_0}}\sqrt\gamma\,M_e\left(1 + \frac{\gamma - 1}{2}M_e^2\right)^{-(\gamma+1)/(2(\gamma-1))} .$$

(A valve "chocked" with a wedge is a different thing entirely.)

Section 10.7Worked examples

Worked examples

A nozzle is to produce M = 3 from a reservoir at 1.0 MPa and 300 K. Find Ae/At, the design back pressure and the exit temperature.

Solution

A/A* = 4.235 at M = 3. pe = p₀/36.73 = 27.2 kPa. Te = 300/2.8 = 107.1 K (cold enough that real tunnels heat the air to avoid condensation).

A nozzle has Ae/At = 2.5 and a sonic throat. Find the two possible isentropic exit Mach numbers and the corresponding pb/p₀.

Solution

Subsonic branch: Me = 0.240, pe/p₀ = 0.961. Supersonic branch: Me = 2.44, pe/p₀ = 0.0640. Between them (0.961 > pb/p₀ > 0.435) a normal shock sits inside the nozzle; 0.435 is the value with the shock exactly at the exit.

Find the mass flow through a choked throat of 20 cm² from a reservoir at 700 kPa and 400 K.

Solution

ṁ = 0.0404(700000)(0.002)/√400 = 2.83 kg/s.

Section 10.8Check your understanding

Sketch M(x) for a C–D nozzle with a sonic throat and a fully supersonic, isentropic diverging section.

Answer

M rises from near 0 in the reservoir, passes 1 exactly at the throat, and keeps rising to Me at the exit; p, T and ρ fall monotonically; p₀ and T₀ are constant.

A converging nozzle exhausts to pb/p₀ = 0.3. What is the exit Mach number and exit pressure?

Answer

Choked: Me = 1, pe = 0.528p₀ > pb (underexpanded; the jet expands outside).

Why must the flow be sonic at a minimum-area section, not anywhere else?

Answer

dA/A = (M² − 1)du/u: with M = 1 the left side must vanish, so dA = 0.
