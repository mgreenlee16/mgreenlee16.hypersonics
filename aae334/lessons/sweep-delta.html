Sweep, Delta Wings and Wingtip Devices

Lifting-line theory assumes a straight, slender wing. Real aircraft sweep their wings, fly delta planforms, add winglets and deploy flaps and slats. This lesson covers what each does qualitatively (the material of Part I questions), how a delta wing makes lift with leading-edge vortices, and the airplane drag polar that sets L/D and stall speed.

Section 4.1Stall and high-lift devices (review)

Airfoils stall in three ways. Trailing-edge stall (thick sections such as the NACA 4421): separation starts at the trailing edge and creeps forward, so the lift curve rounds over gently. Leading-edge stall (moderately thick sections such as the 4412): a short laminar separation bubble near the nose bursts and the lift drops abruptly. Thin-airfoil stall (flat plates, very thin sections): a long leading-edge bubble grows with α until it covers the chord. cℓ,max rises with thickness up to about 12–15% and then falls.

In level flight L = W, so

$$V_\infty = \sqrt{\frac{2W}{\rho_\infty S C_L}},\qquad V_\text{stall} = \sqrt{\frac{2W}{\rho_\infty S C_{L,\max}}} .$$

A higher CL,max lowers the stall speed, shortening takeoff and landing. Trailing-edge flaps add camber (and with Fowler flaps, area): the lift curve shifts up and left (αL=0 more negative) with about the same slope, and CL,max rises, but the stall angle falls slightly. Leading-edge slats and Krueger flaps delay leading-edge separation: the lift curve extends to a much higher stall angle (the 4412 goes from about 15° to near 30°) with little change at low α. Multi-element wings get very high cℓ because high-momentum air through each slot gives the next element a fresh boundary layer. By convention, coefficients still use the clean wing area. Typical values: cℓ,max ≈ 1.3–1.7 clean, 2.5–3.5 with flaps and slats.

Section 4.2Wingtip devices

A wing needs circulation to lift, and trailing vorticity cannot be eliminated, only redistributed. Span is limited by structural weight and airport gates, so designers try to move the tip vorticity outward or spread it.

  • Non-lifting end plates block the flow around the tip but add viscous drag; they are not effective on airplanes (they are useful in wind tunnels to simulate 2-D flow).
  • Lifting winglets act like a small span extension: they move the vortex outward and partly cancel downwash. Reported induced-drag reductions are a few percent (fuel savings of several percent on some retrofits). Drawbacks: weight, root bending moment, interference drag (avoid lining up the Cp,min of wing and winglet, which lowers the critical Mach number) and ground or gate clearance.
  • Raked tips, blended, split-scimitar and spiroid winglets trade the same benefits against clearance and weight.

Section 4.3Effects of wing sweep

Sweep angle Λ is usually measured at the quarter-chord line. Lifting-line theory no longer applies; Weissinger's method (a vortex lattice with one chordwise panel) or a full vortex-lattice method is needed. Know at least five effects of aft sweep:

  1. Raises the critical Mach number: the wing responds mainly to the velocity component normal to its leading edge, V cos Λ (simple sweep theory).
  2. Reduces the magnitude of the minimum pressure coefficient for the same reason.
  3. Reduces the lift slope and the lift coefficient at a given α.
  4. Loads the tips: the bound vortex of each half-wing induces downwash on the other near the root, unloading it, so the spanwise cℓ peaks outboard: tip stall.
  5. Drives the boundary layer outboard: a 3-D boundary layer with spanwise flow toward the tips thickens the tip boundary layer and promotes tip stall (it is also less stable at low Reynolds number).
  6. Tip stall moves the center of pressure forward (the tips of an aft-swept wing are behind the root), giving a nose-up pitch break that hurts stability.

Forward sweep reverses the induced effects: each half-wing gets upwash from the other near the root, the root is loaded and stalls first (keeping aileron control), and the boundary layer flows inboard. But root stall moves the center of pressure forward too, and forward-swept wings are prone to aeroelastic divergence (X-29, HFB-320 Hansa Jet).

Section 4.4Mitigating tip stall

Three standard strategies, which you should be able to recognize on real airplanes:

  • Twist (washout): geometric or aerodynamic twist that lowers the tip cℓ (Lesson 3).
  • Vortex generators: small vanes that mix high-momentum air into the boundary layer and delay separation, at a small drag penalty (also micro vortex generators and active versions: synthetic jets and plasma actuators).
  • Boundary-layer fences: chordwise plates that block the outward spanwise boundary-layer flow on swept wings (MiG-15, Caravelle; a dogtooth or a saw-cut leading edge does the same job aerodynamically, as on the F-4).

Section 4.5Beyond lifting line: vortex lattice and panel methods

A vortex-lattice method covers the planform with panels, each carrying a horseshoe vortex whose bound segment lies on the panel's quarter-chord line, with a control point at the three-quarter chord. Flow tangency at N control points gives N equations for the N vortex strengths. Camber can be included by tilting the boundary condition; thickness cannot. A 3-D panel method (Hess–Smith) puts sources and doublets on the actual surface and does capture thickness. Programs such as XFLR5/FLOW5 use these methods.

Section 4.6Delta wings and leading-edge vortices

Delta wings (Concorde, F-102, the Space Shuttle, many fighters) are used for supersonic flight: the highly swept leading edge stays inside the Mach cone, and the long root chord gives low thickness ratio with deep structure. They still have to land, and at low speed they behave very differently from a high-aspect-ratio wing.

At moderate α the flow separates at the sharp, swept leading edge. The shear layer rolls up into a pair of strong, stable leading-edge vortices lying above the upper surface. Their low-pressure cores produce large suction peaks on the outboard upper surface, so the spanwise Cp distribution has two suction humps. This adds a nonlinear vortex lift to the ordinary (potential) lift. Polhamus's leading-edge suction analogy writes

$$C_L = K_p\sin\alpha\cos^2\alpha + K_v\cos\alpha\sin^2\alpha,$$

where the first term is the potential lift and the second the vortex lift (Kv ≈ π for slender deltas). Compared with a high-AR wing, a delta wing has:

  • a smaller lift slope (low aspect ratio, AR = 4/tan ΛLE);
  • a much higher stall angle (often 30–40°);
  • about the same CL,max.

The low slope means very nose-high landing attitudes (Concorde's drooping nose, the F-14's variable sweep). L/D is poor: a rounded leading edge recovers some suction at low speed but is impractical supersonically; leading-edge vortex flaps redirect the vortex suction forward to cut drag.

Section 4.7Vortex breakdown and delta-wing stall

A leading-edge vortex has a strong axial jet in its core. As α rises, the adverse pressure gradient along the core grows until, at some point, the core suddenly stagnates, expands into a larger, turbulent, slower swirling region: vortex breakdown (bubble or spiral type). Downstream of breakdown the vortex no longer produces strong suction.

At low α breakdown happens in the wake, behind the trailing edge. As α increases the breakdown point moves upstream over the wing. Lift keeps growing until breakdown has moved well forward; when it approaches the apex the vortex lift collapses and the wing stalls. That is why delta-wing stall is gradual and occurs at a high angle (around 35° for a 70–75° delta). Asymmetric breakdown can cause abrupt roll; breakdown over the tails of the F/A-18 and F-16 causes buffeting.

Section 4.8The airplane drag polar and L/D

For a whole airplane, a good approximation is the parabolic polar

$$C_D = C_{D,0} + KC_L^2,\qquad K = \frac{1}{\pi e AR},$$

where CD,0 is the zero-lift (parasite) drag of everything and e is the Oswald efficiency factor, lower than the wing's span efficiency because it also absorbs the variation of profile drag with lift. A common curve fit for straight-wing aircraft (Raymer) is \(e = 1.78(1 - 0.045AR^{0.68}) - 0.64\). The lift-to-drag ratio \(L/D = C_L/(C_{D,0} + KC_L^2)\) is largest where \(d(L/D)/dC_L = 0\):

$$C_L^* = \sqrt{\frac{C_{D,0}}{K}},\qquad \left(\frac LD\right)_{\max} = \frac{1}{2\sqrt{KC_{D,0}}} = \frac12\sqrt{\frac{\pi eAR}{C_{D,0}}},$$

at the point where induced drag equals zero-lift drag. Higher aspect ratio and lower parasite drag both raise (L/D)max, which sets glide ratio and, for jets, best range.

Section 4.9Key facts and equations

Facts to know

Aft sweep: higher Mcr, lower |Cp,min|, lower lift slope and CL, tip loading, outboard boundary-layer flow, tip stall, forward shift of c.p.

Tip-stall fixes: washout, vortex generators, fences.

Delta vs high-AR: smaller slope, higher αstall, similar CL,max; stall by vortex breakdown reaching the apex.

Equations

$$V_\text{stall} = \sqrt{\frac{2W}{\rho S C_{L,\max}}}$$ $$C_D = C_{D,0} + \frac{C_L^2}{\pi eAR}$$ $$C_L^* = \sqrt{C_{D,0}\pi eAR},\quad \left(\frac LD\right)_{\max} = \frac12\sqrt{\frac{\pi eAR}{C_{D,0}}}$$ $$C_L = K_p\sin\alpha\cos^2\alpha + K_v\cos\alpha\sin^2\alpha$$

Section 4.10Worked examples

Worked examples

A business jet has CD,0 = 0.021, AR = 8.5 and e = 0.80. Find K, CL* and (L/D)max.

Solution

K = 1/(π·0.8·8.5) = 0.04681. CL* = √(0.021/0.04681) = 0.670. (L/D)max = 1/(2√(0.04681·0.021)) = 15.95. At that point CD = 2CD,0 = 0.042 and 0.670/0.042 = 15.95 ✓.

A 1100 kg airplane has S = 16.2 m². Clean CL,max = 1.5; with full flaps 2.1. Find both stall speeds at sea level.

Solution

V = √(2·10787/(1.225·16.2·CL,max)) = √(1087/CL,max): 26.9 m/s clean and 22.8 m/s with flaps (52 and 44 knots). The ratio is √(1.5/2.1) = 0.845.

On one set of axes, sketch CL vs α for a 70° delta wing and an AR 8 straight wing with the same zero-lift angle.

Solution

Both lines start at the same αL=0. The straight wing is steeper (≈ 4.9 rad⁻¹) and stalls near 14–16° at CL,max ≈ 1.3–1.4. The delta starts with a shallow slope (≈ 2 rad⁻¹), curves upward as vortex lift builds, and peaks near 35° at a similar CL,max, then falls off gradually. Use Interactive 2 to check your sketch.

Name the device: (a) chordwise plates on the upper surface of a swept wing; (b) a row of small tabs near the leading edge of the outboard wing; (c) a vertical surface at the wingtip; (d) the outboard wing set at a lower incidence than the root.

Solution

(a) boundary-layer fences (block spanwise flow, against tip stall); (b) vortex generators (energize the boundary layer, against tip stall); (c) a winglet (reduce induced drag); (d) washout (against tip stall).

Section 4.11Check your understanding

List five effects of sweeping a wing aft.

Answer

Higher critical Mach number; smaller |Cp,min|; lower lift slope and CL; higher tip loading (tip stall); outboard spanwise boundary-layer flow; forward c.p. shift at stall.

Why do forward-swept wings stall at the root?

Answer

The bound vortex of each half induces upwash on the other near the centerline, loading the root, and the boundary layer flows inboard.

Sketch the spanwise Cp on the upper surface of a delta wing at α = 20°.

Answer

Two strong suction peaks, one under each leading-edge vortex, outboard near the leading edges, with weaker suction in between.

What ends the lift growth of a delta wing?

Answer

Vortex breakdown moving forward from the wake over the wing; near the apex the vortex suction is lost and the wing stalls.
