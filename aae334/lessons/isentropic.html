Energy Equation and Isentropic Flow

Bring a moving gas to rest and its temperature, pressure and density rise. How much depends only on the Mach number. This lesson derives the stagnation (total) relations from the energy equation and the isentropic relations of Lesson 6, finds the speed of sound, defines the sonic reference state, and answers the question "when is a flow compressible?".

Section 7.1The energy equation

Apply the first law to a steady control volume (a stream tube) between stations 1 and 2, with no heat transfer (adiabatic), no shaft work and no body forces. The energy carried in by the flow is the enthalpy (internal energy plus flow work) plus the kinetic energy, so

$$h_1 + \frac{u_1^2}{2} = h_2 + \frac{u_2^2}{2} \equiv h_0 = \text{const}.$$

\(h_0\) is the stagnation (total) enthalpy: the enthalpy the gas would have if brought to rest adiabatically. Note what was not assumed: the result holds even if there is friction or a shock inside the control volume, as long as no heat or work crosses its boundary. (This is effectively the compressible Bernoulli equation, but in this form it involves h, which depends on p and ρ, not p and ρ separately.)

Section 7.2The speed of sound

Derivation: a weak pressure wave

A weak compression wave moves at speed a into still gas (p, ρ). Ride with the wave: the gas arrives at speed a and leaves at a − du, compressed to p + dp, ρ + dρ, where du is the velocity the wave gives the gas in the lab frame (in the direction the wave travels).

$$\text{mass: } \rho a = (\rho + d\rho)(a - du)\ \Rightarrow\ du = \frac{a}{\rho}d\rho,\qquad \text{momentum: } p + \rho a^2 = (p + dp) + \rho a(a - du)\ \Rightarrow\ dp = \rho a\,du .$$

Eliminating du gives \(dp = a^2d\rho\). A sound wave is so weak that it is reversible and adiabatic: isentropic. So

$$a^2 = \left(\frac{\partial p}{\partial\rho}\right)_s = \frac{\gamma p}{\rho} = \gamma RT .$$

The speed of sound depends on temperature alone (for a given gas), and it is of the same order as the mean molecular speed, since sound is carried by molecular collisions. At sea level (288.15 K) \(a = \sqrt{1.4(287)(288.15)} = 340.3\) m/s = 1116 ft/s = 761 mph, about a fifth of a mile per second: count the seconds after lightning and divide by five for the distance in miles. The Mach number \(M = u/a\) compares the directed motion of the gas with the random thermal motion of its molecules.

Section 7.3Stagnation temperature

For a calorically perfect gas, \(h = c_pT\), so the energy equation reads \(c_pT_0 = c_pT + u^2/2\). Divide by cpT and use \(c_p = \gamma R/(\gamma - 1)\) and \(a^2 = \gamma RT\):

$$\frac{T_0}{T} = 1 + \frac{u^2}{2c_pT} = 1 + \frac{(\gamma - 1)u^2}{2\gamma RT}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad \boxed{\;\frac{T_0}{T} = 1 + \frac{\gamma - 1}{2}M^2\;}$$

This needs only adiabatic flow, so T₀ is constant through shocks and friction too. Since T₀ is fixed, the static temperature falls as the Mach number rises.

Section 7.4Stagnation pressure and density

If the deceleration is also reversible (isentropic), Lesson 6's relations apply between the static and the stagnation state:

$$\boxed{\;\frac{p_0}{p} = \left(1 + \frac{\gamma - 1}{2}M^2\right)^{\gamma/(\gamma - 1)},\qquad \frac{\rho_0}{\rho} = \left(1 + \frac{\gamma - 1}{2}M^2\right)^{1/(\gamma - 1)}\;}$$

For air the exponents are 3.5 and 2.5. These are the equations worth memorizing.

Stagnation conditions are local propertiesEven if the actual flow is not isentropic, p₀, T₀ and ρ₀ can be defined at every point as the values the gas would reach if brought to rest isentropically from its local state. T₀ stays constant in any adiabatic flow; p₀ stays constant only along isentropic paths and drops across shocks. For a compressible flow, compute p₀ with this relation, never with Bernoulli's equation.

Section 7.5Sonic (critical) conditions

A second reference state is the one reached by accelerating or decelerating the flow isentropically to M = 1, denoted by an asterisk. It occurs in practice at a nozzle throat. Setting M = 1:

$$\frac{T^*}{T_0} = \frac{2}{\gamma + 1} = 0.8333,\qquad \frac{p^*}{p_0} = \left(\frac{2}{\gamma + 1}\right)^{\gamma/(\gamma-1)} = 0.5283,\qquad \frac{\rho^*}{\rho_0} = \left(\frac{2}{\gamma + 1}\right)^{1/(\gamma-1)} = 0.6339,$$

(numbers for γ = 1.4) and \(a^* = \sqrt{\gamma RT^*}\). The characteristic Mach number \(M^* = u/a^*\) is sometimes used; it tends to \(\sqrt{(\gamma+1)/(\gamma-1)} = 2.449\) as M → ∞. The inverse \(p_0/p^* = 1.893\) appears again for Pitot probes (Lesson 9) and nozzle choking (Lesson 10). A tank discharging through a small hole into an atmosphere at pb has sonic (choked) flow in the hole whenever \(p_b/p_0 \le 0.528\).

Section 7.6Trends with Mach number

For isentropic flow, as the Mach number increases (flow accelerates and expands): static pressure, temperature, density and the speed of sound decrease; velocity increases; stagnation pressure, temperature and density and the entropy stay the same. These trends make physical sense and are your best tool for spotting an error in a solution.

Section 7.7When is a flow compressible?

At a stagnation point the density rises by \(\rho_0/\rho_\infty = (1 + 0.2M^2)^{2.5}\): 1% at M = 0.14, 5% at M = 0.31, 10% at M = 0.44. That is the origin of the M > 0.3 rule. In terms of pressure, expand \(p_0/p\) in a Taylor series in M²:

$$p_0 - p_\infty = \tfrac12\rho_\infty V_\infty^2\left(1 + \frac{M^2}{4} + \frac{2 - \gamma}{24}M^4 + \cdots\right).$$

The first term is Bernoulli; the bracket is the compressibility correction, under 2.5% at M = 0.3 and about 17% at M = 0.8. Viewed another way, bringing a high-Mach-number flow to rest converts its large kinetic energy into internal energy: at M = 5 the stagnation temperature is six times the static value.

Section 7.8Using the isentropic table

The isentropic table (Appendix A of Anderson, and the tables page here) lists p/p₀, ρ/ρ₀, T/T₀ and A/A* against M. Given any one ratio you can read M, and from M every other ratio. On the exam, do not interpolate: use the entry closest to the value you need, being careful to pick the closest one. Typical chains:

  • Given T₀, p₀ and M: T = T₀(T/T₀), p = p₀(p/p₀), ρ = p/(RT), a = √(γRT), u = Ma, h = cpT, h₀ = cpT₀.
  • Given p and p₀ (e.g., a Pitot reading in subsonic flow): p/p₀ → M → T/T₀.
  • Given T and u: M = u/√(γRT), then T₀ and p₀.

Entropy is the same at every point of an isentropic flow; s − sref = cpln(T/Tref) − R ln(p/pref) gives the same answer at any station.

Section 7.9Key equations

Energy and sound

$$h_0 = h + \tfrac12u^2 = c_pT_0,\qquad a = \sqrt{\gamma RT},\qquad M = u/a$$ $$\frac{T_0}{T} = 1 + \frac{\gamma - 1}{2}M^2\ \ (\text{adiabatic})$$

Isentropic

$$\frac{p_0}{p} = \left(\frac{T_0}{T}\right)^{\frac{\gamma}{\gamma-1}},\qquad \frac{\rho_0}{\rho} = \left(\frac{T_0}{T}\right)^{\frac1{\gamma-1}}$$ $$\frac{T^*}{T_0} = 0.8333,\quad \frac{p^*}{p_0} = 0.5283,\quad \frac{\rho^*}{\rho_0} = 0.6339$$

Section 7.10Worked examples

Worked examples

An airplane flies at 830 ft/s at 30,000 ft, where p = 4.37 psia and T = 411.8 °R. Use cp = 6006 ft·lbf/(slug·°R). Find M, h₀, T₀ and p₀.

Solution

a = √(1.4·1716·411.8) = 994.7 ft/s; M = 0.8344. h₀ = 6006(411.8) + ½(830)² = 2.473×10⁶ + 3.445×10⁵ = 2.818×10⁶ ft²/s². T₀ = 411.8(1 + 0.2·0.6962) = 469.1 °R (= h₀/cp ✓). p₀ = 4.37(1.13924)3.5 = 4.37(1.5786) = 6.90 psia.

Air from a reservoir at p₀ = 800 kPa, T₀ = 450 K expands isentropically to M = 2.2. Find p, T, ρ, u and the entropy change.

Solution

T₀/T = 1 + 0.2(4.84) = 1.968 → T = 228.7 K. p₀/p = 1.9683.5 = 10.69 → p = 74.83 kPa. ρ = 74830/(287·228.7) = 1.140 kg/m³. a = 303.1 m/s, u = 666.9 m/s. Δs = 0 (isentropic). Table at M = 2.20: p/p₀ = 0.09352, T/T₀ = 0.5081, the same values.

For the reservoir of Ex 7.2, find the throat conditions when the throat is sonic.

Solution

T* = 0.8333(450) = 375.0 K; p* = 0.5283(800) = 422.6 kPa; ρ* = 422600/(287·375) = 3.927 kg/m³; u* = a* = √(1.4·287·375) = 388.2 m/s.

At sea level a probe is in a flow at M = 0.7. How much does Bernoulli's equation underestimate p₀ − p∞?

Solution

Exact: p₀/p = (1.098)3.5 = 1.3871, so p₀ − p = 0.3871p. Bernoulli: ½ρV² = ½γpM² = 0.343p. Ratio 0.343/0.3871 = 0.886: Bernoulli is 11.4% low on the pressure difference (the series 1 + M²/4 + M⁴/40 = 1.1285 agrees).

Section 7.11Check your understanding

What assumptions give h₀ = h + u²/2 = constant? Which of them does T₀/T = 1 + 0.2M² add?

Answer

Steady, adiabatic, no shaft work, no body forces (inviscid is often stated, but friction inside the CV does not change h₀). T₀/T adds a calorically perfect gas.

Why does p₀ need "isentropic" while T₀ needs only "adiabatic"?

Answer

T₀ follows from energy alone. p₀ requires the Gibbs relation with ds = 0; irreversibility at the same h₀ lowers p₀ (p₀₂/p₀₁ = e−Δs/R).

Two gases have the same T. Which has the higher speed of sound, helium or air?

Answer

Helium: a = √(γRT) with γ = 5/3 and R = 2077 J/(kg K), about 2.9 times faster than air at the same T.

Above what Mach number is compressibility important, and why?

Answer

About 0.3: the stagnation density is ~5% above the freestream value, and the error in Bernoulli's p₀ − p is about 2%.
