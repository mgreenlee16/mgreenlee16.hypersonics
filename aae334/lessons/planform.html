Taper, Twist and Aspect-Ratio Scaling

Why do we taper wings? How does taper change where a wing stalls first, and why do designers twist the tips down? This lesson applies the lifting-line solution of Lesson 2 to real planforms, introduces Schrenk's quick approximation, and shows how to rescale wind-tunnel data from one aspect ratio to another.

Section 3.1Three ways to get elliptic loading

Write the lifting-line equation as \(\alpha(y) = \Gamma/(\pi V_\infty c) + \alpha_{L=0}(y) + \alpha_i\). With elliptic loading, αi is constant, so the circulation \(\Gamma = \pi V_\infty c\,[\alpha - \alpha_{L=0} - \alpha_i]\) is elliptic whenever the product \(c(y)\,[\alpha(y) - \alpha_{L=0}(y) - \alpha_i]\) is. Three ways achieve it:

  • Elliptic chord \(c(y)\) with constant α and αL=0 (the Spitfire; its quarter-chord line, not its mid-chord, is straight, so it is not drawn as a symmetric ellipse).
  • Geometric twist: constant chord, with α(y) varied so the product is elliptic.
  • Aerodynamic twist: constant chord and α, with the airfoil (and hence αL=0) changing along the span.

Or any combination. All are expensive to build, and a twisted design is elliptic at only one CL. The practical compromise is a straight-tapered wing.

Section 3.2Taper ratio and induced drag

The taper ratio is \(\lambda = c_t/c_r\), tip chord over root chord. λ = 1 is rectangular, λ = 0 a pointed tip, and λ > 1 an impractical "bow-tie". For a straight-tapered wing

$$c(y) = c_r\left[1 - (1-\lambda)\frac{2|y|}{b}\right],\qquad S = \frac{b\,c_r(1+\lambda)}{2},\qquad c_r = \frac{2S}{b(1+\lambda)} .$$

Lifting-line theory gives the induced drag factor δ as a function of λ (Interactive 2). It has a shallow minimum near λ ≈ 0.3–0.35, where δ is under 0.02: a tapered wing comes close to elliptic. A rectangular wing (λ = 1) has δ ≈ 0.05 at AR 6, and an over-tapered wing (λ → 0) is worse than rectangular. So a tapered wing may have larger or smaller induced drag than a rectangular wing of the same AR and CL, depending on λ. Note also that the aspect ratio matters much more than δ: \(C_{D_i} = C_L^2(1+\delta)/(\pi AR)\).

Why taper? Advantages and disadvantagesTaper moves lift (and structure) inboard, which reduces the root bending moment and the wing weight; it puts a deep root where the spar, landing gear and fuel go; and moderate taper lowers the induced drag toward the elliptic value. The costs: the outboard sections run at a higher cℓ, so the tips tend to stall first (with loss of aileron control), small tip chords mean low tip Reynolds numbers, and the manufacturing is more complex than a constant-chord wing.

Section 3.3cℓ(y) versus L′(y), and tip stall

Keep two distributions apart. The span loading \(L'(y) = q_\infty c\,c_\ell\) is the force per unit span: on any normal wing it is largest at the root and falls to zero at the tips. The section lift coefficient \(c_\ell(y) = L'/(q_\infty c)\) is the loading divided by the local chord. It tells you how hard each airfoil section is working, and so where stall starts.

  • Rectangular wing: L′ is fuller than elliptic near the tips but the chord is constant, so cℓ is largest at the root and falls toward the tips. The root stalls first, which is benign (the ailerons keep working, and the separated root flow buffets the tail as a warning).
  • Moderate taper (λ ≈ 0.35–0.5): cℓ is nearly flat, close to elliptic.
  • Strong taper (λ → 0): L′ falls toward the tips, but the chord falls faster, so cℓ peaks outboard. The tips reach cℓ,max first: tip stall, with loss of roll control and a tendency to drop a wing.
  • Elliptic planform: cℓ is constant, so the whole span stalls at once.

Aft sweep loads the tips further (Lesson 4); washout unloads them.

Section 3.4Schrenk's approximation

Schrenk (1940) proposed a quick estimate that needs no lifting-line solution: the span loading of an untwisted wing lies halfway between its chord distribution and an ellipse of the same span and area,

$$L'(y) \approx \frac12\left[c(y) + \frac{4S}{\pi b}\sqrt{1 - \left(\frac{2y}{b}\right)^2}\,\right]\frac{L}{S},\qquad c_\ell(y) \approx \frac{C_L}{2}\left[1 + \frac{4}{\pi}\frac{\bar c}{c(y)}\sqrt{1 - \left(\frac{2y}{b}\right)^2}\,\right],$$

with \(\bar c = S/b\). Both terms integrate to S, so the total lift is right. The second form makes the tip-stall tendency obvious: where the local chord c(y) is small, cℓ is large.

Section 3.5Twisted wings: basic and additional loading

Measure every angle from the zero-lift line. The absolute angle of attack \(\alpha_a = \alpha - \alpha_{L=0}\) of a section splits into two parts:

$$\alpha_a(y) = \alpha_{aw} + \alpha_{ab}(y),$$
  • \(\alpha_{aw}\), the additional absolute angle: the angle of the wing's own zero-lift line, the same for every section. It is the part that changes as the airplane pitches.
  • \(\alpha_{ab}(y)\), the basic absolute angle: the angle from each section's zero-lift line to the wing's zero-lift line. It is fixed by the twist and independent of the wing's attitude.

The lifting-line equation is linear in Γ, so the circulation splits the same way, \(\Gamma = \Gamma_a + \Gamma_b\):

$$\alpha_{aw} = \frac{\Gamma_a}{\pi V_\infty c} + \alpha_i[\Gamma_a],\qquad \alpha_{ab}(y_0) = \frac{\Gamma_b}{\pi V_\infty c} + \alpha_i[\Gamma_b] .$$

The additional loading Γa is the loading of the untwisted wing, scaled by αaw (and so by CL). The basic loading Γb is the loading at zero total lift, \(\int\Gamma_b\,dy = 0\). With washout (tips twisted nose-down) the basic loading is positive inboard and negative at the tips; with wash-in it is the reverse.

Consequences you should be able to explain:

  • Washout lowers the tip cℓ at every lift, so it is the standard fix for tip stall on tapered and swept wings.
  • A twisted wing has induced drag at CL = 0: the basic loading still sheds trailing vorticity (lift up inboard, down at the tips), even though the net lift is zero.
  • Its induced drag polar is not symmetric about CL = 0: \(C_{D_i} = \pi AR\sum n(A_{n,a} + A_{n,b})^2\) has a term linear in CL, so the minimum induced drag occurs at a positive CL for washout, and e depends on CL.

Section 3.6Rescaling data with aspect ratio

Wind-tunnel data are measured on one model; we often need them for another aspect ratio (Prandtl's classic plots rescale many rectangular wings to AR = 5, collapsing them onto one curve). Assume the two wings have the same airfoil, the same profile drag at a given CL, and nearly the same e. At the same CL:

$$C_{D,1} = C_{D,2} + \frac{C_L^2}{\pi e}\left(\frac1{AR_1} - \frac1{AR_2}\right),\qquad \alpha_1 = \alpha_2 + \frac{C_L}{\pi e}\left(\frac1{AR_1} - \frac1{AR_2}\right)\ \ (\text{rad}).$$

The second line follows because the effective angle (and hence the profile drag) is the same at the same CL; only αi changes. Equivalently, the lift slopes scale as

$$\frac{a_1}{a_2} = \frac{1 + a_0/(\pi AR_2)}{1 + a_0/(\pi AR_1)} \xrightarrow{a_0 = 2\pi} \frac{1 + 2/AR_2}{1 + 2/AR_1},$$

so at the same α (measured from αL=0), \(C_{L,1}/C_{L,2} = a_1/a_2\). For a non-elliptic wing, \(a = a_0/[1 + (a_0/\pi AR)(1+\tau)]\).

Limitationse and τ change slightly with AR, and CD0 is really the airfoil cd, which varies with CL (a 4412 section's drag bucket), so the drag polar is only approximately a parabola.

Section 3.7Key equations

Planform

$$\lambda = \frac{c_t}{c_r},\quad c_r = \frac{2S}{b(1+\lambda)},\quad c_\ell = \frac{L'}{q_\infty c}$$ $$L' \approx \tfrac12\left[c + \tfrac{4S}{\pi b}\sqrt{1-(2y/b)^2}\right]\tfrac{L}{S}\ \ (\text{Schrenk})$$ $$\alpha_a = \alpha - \alpha_{L=0} = \alpha_{aw} + \alpha_{ab}(y),\quad \textstyle\int\Gamma_b\,dy = 0$$

Aspect-ratio scaling (same CL)

$$C_{D,1} = C_{D,2} + \frac{C_L^2}{\pi e}\left(\frac1{AR_1} - \frac1{AR_2}\right)$$ $$\alpha_1 = \alpha_2 + \frac{C_L}{\pi e}\left(\frac1{AR_1} - \frac1{AR_2}\right)$$ $$\frac{a_1}{a_2} = \frac{1 + 2/AR_2}{1 + 2/AR_1}\ \ (a_0 = 2\pi)$$

Section 3.8Worked examples

Worked examples

A wing has b = 12 m, S = 18 m² and λ = 0.4. Find AR, cr, ct and the chord at 2y/b = 0.5.

Solution

AR = 144/18 = 8. cr = 2(18)/[12(1.4)] = 2.143 m, ct = 0.857 m. c(0.5) = 2.143[1 − 0.6(0.5)] = 1.500 m (which is also the mean chord S/b, as it must be for a straight taper at mid-semispan).

For the wing of Ex 3.1 at CL = 1.0, use Schrenk to find cℓ at the root and at 2y/b = 0.9.

Solution

c̄ = S/b = 1.5 m and (4/π)c̄ = 1.910 m.

Root: cℓ = 0.5[1 + 1.910/2.143] = 0.5(1.891) = 0.946.

2y/b = 0.9: c = 2.143(1 − 0.54) = 0.986 m; √(1 − 0.81) = 0.436. cℓ = 0.5[1 + 1.910(0.436)/0.986] = 0.5(1.845) = 0.922. Nearly flat, as expected near λ ≈ 0.4. Repeat with λ = 0.1 and the outboard cℓ rises above the root value.

A wing of AR 5 measures CL = 0.8, CD = 0.062 at α = 9.6°. Predict CD and α for an AR 9 wing with the same airfoil at CL = 0.8 (e = 0.95).

Solution

CL²/(πe) = 0.64/2.985 = 0.2144; (1/9 − 1/5) = −0.0889.

CD = 0.062 − 0.2144(0.0889) = 0.0429. Δα = (0.8/2.985)(−0.0889) = −0.0238 rad = −1.37°, so α = 8.2°.

An AR 4 wing (thin airfoil) gives CL = 0.5 at α = 6° (αL=0 = 0). What CL does an AR 12 wing of the same airfoil give at 6°?

Solution

a₁₂/a₄ = (1 + 2/4)/(1 + 2/12) = 1.5/1.1667 = 1.286, so CL = 0.643.

Section 3.9Check your understanding

Sketch cℓ(y) for λ = 0, 0.3 and 1 (untwisted, same AR and CL) and say which stalls first where.

Answer

λ = 1: largest at the root (root stall). λ ≈ 0.3: nearly flat. λ = 0: rises toward the tips (tip stall).

Give three ways to mitigate tip stall.

Answer

Washout (geometric or aerodynamic twist), vortex generators on the outboard wing, and boundary-layer fences. Also less taper or a high-cℓ,max airfoil at the tip, or leading-edge slots/slats outboard.

Why does a wing with washout have induced drag at zero lift?

Answer

Its basic loading is up inboard and down at the tips. The net lift is zero, but Γ(y) still varies, so trailing vortices are shed and there is downwash and induced drag.

True or false: a tapered wing always has less induced drag than a rectangular one of the same AR.

Answer

False. Near λ ≈ 0.3 it has less; at λ → 0 it has more.
